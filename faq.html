Frequently Asked Questions

Honest answers, drawn from published research. Where the science is uncertain or contested, we say so.

Is dream interpretation scientific?

Partly. Research supports the idea that dreams reflect your waking concerns, emotions, and recent experiences (the “continuity hypothesis”), but there is no scientific evidence for fixed, universal symbol meanings. A dream dictionary can be a fun starting point for reflection — not a decoder of objective truth. The most defensible interpretation of any dream is the one grounded in your life and associations.

Does a snake (or falling, or teeth) dream mean the same thing for everyone?

No. Studies find no universal meanings for dream symbols; the same image can mean opposite things to different people depending on culture and personal experience. A snake may evoke fear in one dreamer and healing or transformation in another. Context — your feelings in the dream and your current life situation — matters more than the symbol itself.

Can dreams predict the future?

There is no accepted scientific evidence for prophetic dreams, although 17–38% of people report having had one. Apparent “hits” are well explained by coincidence (we dream thousands of dreams over a lifetime), selective memory for matches, vague content retro-fitted to events, and the brain’s unconscious processing of real-world cues. That said, a dream about an upcoming worry can absolutely reflect real concerns worth paying attention to.

Why do I forget my dreams so quickly?

During REM sleep, the memory-stamping chemical norepinephrine drops to near zero and the hippocampus is under-engaged, so dreams are rarely saved to long-term memory. A 2019 study even found specific neurons that appear to actively suppress dream memories during REM. Waking gently and writing dreams down immediately dramatically improves recall — see our dream recall guide.

Do animals dream?

Almost certainly. Rats in MIT experiments replayed maze runs in their sleep so precisely that scientists could tell where in the maze the rat was “dreaming” it was. Dogs, cats, birds, and possibly octopuses show similar sleep-replay or dream-like behavior. Whether their subjective experience resembles ours remains an open question.

Do blind people dream?

Yes. People who lose sight after about age 5–7 typically keep visual imagery in dreams for life. People blind from birth dream vividly through sound, touch, smell, taste, and emotion — and some research suggests their visual brain areas are still active during dreams.

Do we dream in color or black and white?

Mostly color. Modern studies find color in about half of reported dreams, with black-and-white reported only around 10% of the time — and intriguingly, greyscale reports are more common among people who grew up watching black-and-white television. Many researchers suspect “black-and-white dreaming” was always partly an artifact of how people described their dreams.

How long do dreams actually last?

Roughly as long as they feel: lucid-dreaming experiments show dream time tracks real time closely. Individual dreams run from a few seconds to 20–30 minutes, and you spend about 1.5–2 hours dreaming per night across 4–6 REM periods, the longest near morning. Dreams can feel hours long because memory stitches together highlights.

If you die in a dream, do you die in real life?

No — this is pure myth. In a 2024 survey, 22% of adults reported having dreamed of dying, and all lived to tell the tale. Death in dreams is common during periods of major life change and is usually read as reflecting transition or anxiety, not literal danger.

What is sleep paralysis?

Sleep paralysis happens when REM sleep’s natural muscle paralysis briefly persists while your mind wakes up: you can’t move or speak, and many people experience chest pressure or vivid, frightening hallucinations. Roughly 8% of people experience it at least once (more among students and shift workers). It’s harmless in isolation, but frequent episodes — especially with trauma history or daytime sleepiness — are worth discussing with a doctor.

Why does déjà vu sometimes feel like “I dreamed this”?

Déjà vu is best explained as a brief memory glitch — familiarity firing without the recollection that should accompany it. Sometimes, though, the feeling really does trace to a forgotten dream that loosely resembled the present moment; in one survey about 20% of people linked their déjà vu to dreams. Neither version is evidence of prophecy.

Can food really cause nightmares? What about cheese?

The cheese-nightmare legend doesn’t hold up: in one British study, 200 people ate cheese nightly for a week and nobody had nightmares. Surveys show people believe dairy, spicy food, and sweets cause strange dreams, but the evidence is weak and self-reported. What’s better established: heavy or late meals can fragment sleep, and fragmented sleep means more remembered (and more vivid) dreams.

Can medications change my dreams?

Yes — this is well documented. SSRIs suppress dreaming while taken but can trigger intense dreams on withdrawal; beta-blockers, melatonin supplements, GLP-1 drugs, and some antibiotics are also linked to vivid dreams or nightmares. Never stop a medication because of dreams without talking to your prescriber.

Are nightmares ever a medical issue?

Occasional nightmares are completely normal — about 85% of adults have at least one a year. But if nightmares occur frequently, cause dread of sleeping, or impair your days, you may have nightmare disorder (affecting roughly 2–6% of adults) — and recurring nightmares affect the majority of people with PTSD. Effective treatments exist, especially Imagery Rehearsal Therapy, so persistent distressing dreams are a reason to see a professional, not just endure. See our nightmares guide.

Does everyone dream — even people who “never dream”?

Virtually everyone with normal REM sleep dreams every night; “non-dreamers” are overwhelmingly people who don’t recall their dreams, which is the brain’s default because dream memories aren’t consolidated. Recall varies hugely between people and improves with interest, journaling, and waking during or just after REM.
