Nightmares & Night Terrors: Causes and Evidence-Based Treatment

Guide 10 min read Based on AASM clinical guidance

Roughly 85% of adults have at least one nightmare a year — completely normal. But frequent nightmares that poison your sleep or your days are a recognized, treatable condition, and the best treatment is neither medication nor “just ignore them.”

Nightmares vs. night terrors

These get confused constantly, and they are different events from different parts of the night:

  • Nightmares happen in REM sleep, mostly in the second half of the night. You wake fully, remember a vivid frightening dream, and can usually recount it. Mostly an adult-and-child phenomenon alike.
  • Night terrors (sleep terrors) erupt from deep non-REM sleep in the first third of the night. The person — usually a child — may scream, bolt upright, and look terrified, but isn’t having a remembered dream and typically recalls nothing in the morning. Affecting roughly 1–6.5% of children, they are distressing to witness, usually harmless, and overwhelmingly outgrown. Management is mostly safety (clear the floor, secure stairs) and, for predictable episodes, scheduled awakenings shortly before the usual time.

What causes nightmares

Stress and anxiety lead the list — the threat-simulation view of dreaming suggests the nightmare system over-fires under real-world pressure. Other established triggers: trauma (recurring nightmares affect the majority of people with PTSD, versus about 5% of the general population), sleep deprivation, irregular schedules, fever, and a well-documented roster of medications — SSRIs (especially on withdrawal), beta-blockers, melatonin, and others. Never stop a medication over dreams without talking to your prescriber.

When is it a disorder?

Clinicians look for three things: nightmares are frequent, they cause distress or dread of sleeping, and they impair your days (fatigue, mood, concentration). Nightmare disorder affects roughly 2–6% of adults. Red flags that deserve a medical conversation regardless: acting out dreams physically (possible REM sleep behavior disorder), gasping or breathing pauses (possible sleep apnea), and severe daytime sleepiness.

Imagery Rehearsal Therapy: the first-line treatment

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine’s position paper recommends Imagery Rehearsal Therapy (IRT) for nightmare disorder and PTSD-associated nightmares. It is disarmingly simple:

  1. Write down the recurring nightmare while awake, in daylight, when it has no power.
  2. Rescript it. Change the narrative any way you like — the ending, the middle, the pursuer’s face. The new version doesn’t need to be realistic; it needs to be yours.
  3. Rehearse the new version for 10–20 minutes a day, vividly imagining it.

Over weeks, the rehearsed script tends to displace the old one — the nightmare either changes or fades. IRT has the strongest evidence base of any nightmare treatment; clinicians may also use CBT variants, exposure-based ERRT, relaxation training, hypnosis, lucid dreaming therapy, or (for PTSD nightmares) the medication prazosin. Notably, the AASM specifically recommends against certain drugs (clonazepam and venlafaxine) for this purpose — another reason treatment decisions belong with a clinician.

What you can do tonight

  • Stabilize the basics. Nightmares feed on sleep deprivation and irregular schedules — the sleep hygiene fundamentals are the foundation every treatment builds on.
  • Journal them. Logging nightmares in your dream journal (the nightmare flag exists for this) reveals patterns and is the raw material for IRT rescripting.
  • Try a rescript. Take your most recent nightmare and rewrite its second half. Read your version before bed.
  • Wind down off-screen. An anxious doomscroll is exactly the pre-sleep cognition you don’t want simulated back at you at 3 a.m.

When to get help: nightmares more than once a week, fear of going to sleep, nightmares that began after trauma, acting out dreams, or breathing irregularities at night. These are treatable — a primary-care doctor, sleep specialist, or therapist trained in IRT/CBT is the right next step. This guide is educational, not medical advice.
