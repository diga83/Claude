# Phatic-Only Slang and the Limits of Meaning-Based Lexical Theory: A Sociolinguistic Review of the "6-7" Meme

## Abstract

In late 2025, Dictionary.com selected "67" (typically rendered *6-7* and pronounced *six-seven*) as its Word of the Year, accompanying the announcement with a striking concession: "Perhaps the most defining feature of '67' is that it's impossible to define" (Dictionary.com, 2025). The term — a non-sequitur interjection that spread explosively among Generation Alpha through TikTok, basketball highlight edits, and a Philadelphia drill song — presents an unusually clean test case for a long-standing tension in the philosophy and linguistics of meaning. Theories that locate lexical meaning in reference, denotation, or truth-conditional contribution struggle to assign *6-7* any content at all; yet the term is demonstrably "meaningful" to its users. This narrative review synthesizes literature across sociolinguistics, semantics and pragmatics, and internet linguistics to argue that *6-7* is best understood as a **phatic-only** expression in the lineage of Malinowski's (1923) "phatic communion" and Jakobson's (1960) "phatic function." Drawing on expressivist semantics (Kaplan, Potts), third-wave sociolinguistic indexicality (Silverstein, Eckert), slang theory (Eble, Halliday), and memetics (Dawkins, McCulloch), the review contends that *6-7* does not so much break meaning-based lexical theory as expose the limits of its scope: a class of items whose communicative value is constituted by social contact, in-group indexing, and affective performance rather than by denotation. The lexicographic paradox of defining the indefinable is, on this reading, not an embarrassment but a data point about what "meaning" in the lexicon can be.

---

## 1. Introduction

The lexicographer's traditional task — pairing a word-form with a definition — presupposes that words have meanings of the kind a definition can capture. Most do. But a recurring challenge to meaning-based lexical theory comes from expressions that resist this pairing: interjections, expletives, discourse particles, greetings, and the formulaic small talk that linguists since Malinowski have grouped under the heading of *phatic* language. These items do real communicative work, yet they are difficult or impossible to define in referential terms.

The viral slang term *6-7* is a near-perfect specimen of this class, and its 2025 elevation to Word of the Year by a dictionary — an institution whose entire enterprise is definition — sharpens the theoretical problem into something approaching paradox. When Dictionary.com's Director of Lexicography, Steve Johnson, says the term is "part inside joke, part social signal and part performance" and that "when people say it, they're not just repeating a meme; they're shouting a feeling" (Smithsonian Magazine, 2025), he is describing an item whose value lies almost entirely outside the denotational dimension that meaning-based theory privileges.

This review pursues four aims. First (§2), it documents the empirical phenomenon: the origins, spread, and reported use of *6-7*. Second (§3), it situates the term within the theory of phatic communication. Third (§4), it lays out the commitments of meaning-based lexical theory — referential, truth-conditional, and lexical-semantic approaches. Fourth (§5–6), it argues that phatic-only slang like *6-7* tests the limits of those approaches, and that the most adequate accounts come from use-based, expressivist, and indexical frameworks. The synthesis throughout is interdisciplinary, treating the meme not as a curiosity but as naturally occurring evidence about the architecture of lexical meaning.

## 2. The "6-7" Phenomenon: Origins, Spread, and Use

### 2.1 Origins

The proximate origin of *6-7* is "Doot Doot (6 7)," a Philadelphia drill song by the rapper **Skrilla**, produced by 1Ellis. The track was unofficially released to streaming platforms in December 2024 and officially issued via Priority Records on **February 7, 2025**, later appearing as a bonus track on the deluxe edition of Skrilla's *Zombie Love Kensington Paradise* (Wikipedia, "Doot Doot (6 7)"; Album of the Year). The song's repeated hook — "6-7" — is the seed of the entire phenomenon.

Crucially for the argument of this review, the phrase was **born semantically underdetermined**. Skrilla has declined to fix its meaning, reportedly saying "I never put an actual meaning on it, and I still would not want to," and elsewhere describing it as representing "what comes up in my head" (Complex, 2025; WHYY, 2025). Proposed sources — a reference to a Philadelphia or Chicago street, or, per linguist Taylor Jones, the police ten-code *10-67* used to report a death — remain speculative (CNN, 2025; Wikipedia, "6-7 (meme)"). The indeterminacy is not a gap in our knowledge; it is constitutive of the term.

### 2.2 Spread

The phrase migrated from music to mass meme through **basketball**. Highlight edits paired the lyric with footage of players listed at six-foot-seven, most prominently **LaMelo Ball** (2.01 m / 6'7"), exploiting the literal height coincidence (Bleacher Report; Wikipedia, "6-7 (meme)"). On March 31, 2025, a YouTube AAU-basketball video by Cam Wilder featured a young boy, later dubbed the "**67 Kid**," shouting "six seven" with a characteristic palms-up, alternating up-and-down hand gesture; the clip became a primary vector of virality (Wikipedia, "6-7 (meme)"). Overtime Elite player Taylen "TK" Kinney further propagated the gesture and even launched a "67 Water" brand (Wikipedia, "Taylen Kinney"). By autumn 2025 the term saturated NBA and WNBA media, NFL celebrations, and celebrity content — Shaquille O'Neal reportedly joined in while admitting he did not understand it (Wikipedia, "6-7 (meme)"), a telling detail for a term whose use is detached from comprehension.

The scale is quantifiable. Dictionary.com reported that *67* appeared in digital media roughly **six times more frequently in October 2025** than across all of 2024, with searches up more than sixfold since June 2025 (Dictionary.com, 2025; NPR, 2025).

### 2.3 Use and reception

In actual use, *6-7* functions as an **interjection or non-sequitur** — shouted on hearing either number, deployed as a punchline, or chanted communally — rather than as a contentful predicate. It is loosely glossed as "so-so" or "maybe this, maybe that," mirrored by the see-sawing hand gesture, but this gloss is post-hoc and unstable (Dictionary.com, 2025). It is widely classified as "**brainrot**" slang: purposefully nonsensical language built around being in on the absurdity.

Reception data reinforce the in-group/out-group dynamic central to the theoretical analysis below. Teachers have banned the phrase and posted videos tallying its frequency (Sioux Falls science teacher Gabe Dannenbring reported hearing it roughly 75 times in a single day); a Michigan choir teacher's class went viral performing a song stitched from Gen Alpha slang; and on 24 November 2025 UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer was publicly corrected by head teacher Jo Anderson after leading schoolchildren in the "6-7" gesture during a visit to Welland Academy, Peterborough (CNN, 2025; Axios, 2025; IBTimes, 2025). The comedy of these episodes turns precisely on who is and is not "in on the joke" — a sociolinguistic fact, not a semantic one.

## 3. The Phatic Frame: Communion, Contact, and Contentless Talk

### 3.1 Malinowski's phatic communion

The conceptual vocabulary for analyzing contentless-but-functional language originates with the anthropologist **Bronisław Malinowski**, whose 1923 essay "The Problem of Meaning in Primitive Languages" appeared as a supplement to Ogden and Richards's *The Meaning of Meaning*. There Malinowski coined the term, "actuated by the demon of terminological invention," for "**a type of speech in which ties of union are created by a mere exchange of words**" (Malinowski, 1923/1946). Phatic communion, for Malinowski, is the language of "free, aimless, social intercourse" — greetings, remarks on the weather, inquiries about health — in which words "fulfil a social function, and that is their principal aim," being "neither the result of intellectual reflection, nor do they necessarily arouse reflection in the listener."

Two features of Malinowski's formulation matter for *6-7*. First, he construed language as "**a mode of action, rather than as a countersign of thought**" — pragmatic before it is representational. Second, his governing metaphor is *communion*: "the breaking of silence, the communion of words is the first act to establish links of fellowship." Phatic language bonds; it does not inform. This is, almost verbatim, what lexicographers and linguists now say about *6-7*.

### 3.2 Jakobson's phatic function

**Roman Jakobson** formalized the idea within his influential six-function model of communication, presented in "Linguistics and Poetics" (1960). Each function corresponds to a factor in the speech event; the **phatic function** corresponds to *contact*, comprising "messages primarily serving to establish, to prolong, or to discontinue communication, to check whether the channel works" — exemplified by "Hello, do you hear me?" and "Are you listening?" (Jakobson, 1960). Jakobson explicitly credited Malinowski for the term and made two further claims of interest here: that the phatic function is "the first verbal function acquired by infants," who communicate before they can exchange information, and that it is the only function humans share with talking birds (Jakobson, 1960; Elcomblus).

The developmental observation resonates with sociolinguist **Valerie Fridland's** analysis of *6-7* as the developmentally normal creation of socially meaningful language by adolescents at a life stage when identity construction is paramount — "not brain rot" but "linguistically normal" (Fridland, 2026, *The Conversation*). The phatic function, in other words, is not a degenerate or impoverished use of language; it is foundational.

### 3.3 Phatic culture online

The phatic frame has been extended to digital communication. **Vincent Miller's** "New Media, Networking and Phatic Culture" (2008) argues that network sociality has produced an online communicative culture "dominated by non-dialogic, non-informational" exchange, in which "the connection, not the content, is what matters" (Miller, 2008). Subsequent work analyzes practices such as Snapchat "streaks" — daily exchanges whose entire point is maintaining a connection counter, independent of any message content — as paradigmatically phatic (see Miller, 2008, and subsequent phatic-media research). A meme that spreads as a shouted gesture, propagating connection rather than proposition, is the lexical-item-scale instance of exactly this phenomenon.

## 4. Meaning-Based Lexical Theory and Its Commitments

To assess what *6-7* "challenges," we need the commitments of the meaning-based tradition in view.

### 4.1 Referential and truth-conditional semantics

The dominant strand of formal semantics ties meaning to the world. **Frege's** (1892) distinction between *Sinn* (sense, a "mode of presentation") and *Bedeutung* (reference) was designed to handle puzzles like the informativeness of "Cicero is Tully" versus "Cicero is Cicero," but it keeps reference central: "the morning star" and "the evening star" differ in sense yet share a referent, Venus (Frege, 1892). **Truth-conditional semantics**, developed by **Donald Davidson** in "Truth and Meaning" (1967) on the back of **Tarski's** formal theory of truth, holds that to give the meaning of a sentence is to give its truth conditions — the circumstances under which it would be true (Davidson, 1967). On this view a word's meaning is its *contribution to the truth conditions* of the sentences in which it occurs.

The difficulty is immediate: *6-7* has no plausible truth-conditional contribution. It is not a predicate, names nothing, and a sentence is no more or less true for containing it. By the lights of strict truth-conditional semantics, it is not so much false as **not truth-apt** — outside the system entirely.

### 4.2 Lexical semantics

**Lexical semantics** studies word meaning and the sense relations — synonymy, hyponymy, antonymy, polysemy — that structure the lexicon (Jurafsky & Martin, ch. 19; Cruse). The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* notes that identifying lexical meaning purely with reference makes it hard to account for synonymy, analytic entailment, and the ambiguity/polysemy distinction, and distinguishes an **inferential** aspect of lexical competence (relations among senses) from a **referential** aspect (naming and application) (SEP, "Word Meaning"). *6-7* enters into neither set of relations robustly: it has no synonyms, no hypernym, no entailments. There is no *sense* for the inferential machinery to operate on. This is why a dictionary can simultaneously crown it and confess it cannot define it.

### 4.3 Use-based alternatives

The principal rival tradition makes *use*, not reference, primary. **Wittgenstein's** *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) furnishes the slogan: "For a large class of cases — though not for all — in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: **the meaning of a word is its use in the language**" (§43, Anscombe translation; the 2009 Hacker–Schulte revision reads "can be explained in this way" for "can be defined thus," but the final clause is identical). Meaning resides in **language-games** "consisting of language and the actions into which it is woven," with category membership organized by **family resemblance** rather than shared essence (Wittgenstein, 1953; SEP, "Wittgenstein"). Notably, Wittgenstein hedged — "not for all" cases — and *6-7* falls squarely in the use-governed, non-ostensive class he had in mind.

Three further use-based frameworks bear directly on slang. **Firth's** (1957) distributional dictum — "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" — grounds meaning in co-occurrence patterns. **Usage-based linguistics** (Bybee; Tomasello) models grammar as a network of **constructions** — conventionalized form-meaning pairings — learned from actual use, and **construction grammar** (Goldberg, 1995) treats lexical and syntactic units alike as form-meaning pairs whose properties may not be predictable from their parts. These frameworks accommodate an item like *6-7* more comfortably, because they permit "meaning" to be a conventionalized pairing of a form with a *function* — including a purely social function — rather than with a referent.

### 4.4 Non-referential items as a standing problem

Crucially, *6-7* is not *sui generis*; it joins a class that formal semantics has long had to handle at its margins. **Discourse markers** are traditionally analyzed as non-truth-conditional: removing "so," "after all," or "you know" does not change a sentence's truth value (Schourup; Blakemore). Relevance Theory treats such connectives as encoding **procedural** rather than conceptual meaning (Blakemore, 2002). And **interjections** are characterized in the expressive-meaning literature as content that "is not truth-conditional in any way." The existence of this margin is the wedge: meaning-based theory has always needed a supplementary account for items whose meaning is not denotational. *6-7* simply pushes that supplement to the center.

## 5. The Challenge: Phatic-Only Slang Against Meaning-Based Theory

This section synthesizes the convergent verdict of linguists who have commented on *6-7* and the theoretical resources that account for it.

### 5.1 The convergent expert verdict: void of denotation, rich in social function

Independent linguists have reached the same conclusion using overlapping vocabulary:

- **Taylor Jones** (University of Pennsylvania): *6-7* is "Semantically … almost void," but "Socially … it carries a communicative function of being a **shibboleth**" — a phrase marking in-group membership while appearing empty to outsiders. He attributes its emptiness to "**semantic bleaching**," whereby a phrase loses its original context and meaning while remaining socially functional (CNN, 2025).
- **Valerie Fridland** (University of Nevada, Reno): the term is "void of any semantic meaning" and does not even refer to a quantity, yet "makes sense" because it carries *social* rather than referential meaning (Fridland, 2026).
- **Adam Aleksic** (the "Etymology Nerd"): "Six-seven doesn't need to have a meaning. What matters is that it lands and provokes a reaction" (Yahoo/Aleksic, 2025).
- **Cynthia Gordon** (Georgetown): the trend "seems oriented to humor and play," but "is only humorous … if a group of people are 'insiders' and collectively evaluate it as funny" — outsiders (teachers) are marked precisely by not being in on it (Georgetown, 2025).
- **Steve Johnson** (Dictionary.com): an interjection, "a burst of energy that spreads and connects people long before anyone agrees on what it actually means" (Smithsonian, 2025).

The agreement is notable: every analyst relocates the term's value from *denotation* to *social contact, identity, and affect* — that is, to the phatic and expressive dimensions.

### 5.2 Semantic bleaching at internet speed

*Semantic bleaching* — the loss of denotational content while form and function persist — is a well-attested diachronic process (e.g., the weakening of "literally," or grammaticalization clines). What *6-7* illustrates is bleaching compressed from centuries into months: the term arguably entered circulation already bleached, its tenuous link to a song lyric and a height statistic severed almost immediately (Etymology Explained; CNN, 2025). A comparable, slightly earlier case is **"yeet,"** which University of Colorado linguists analyzed as undergoing "indexical bleaching" as it spread from African American English and became attached primarily to gesture and stance (CU Boulder, 2021). Internet circulation functions, in effect, as a high-speed laboratory for processes that historical linguistics usually observes only in slow motion — and it produces items that arrive at the lexicon already stripped of the content meaning-based theory needs.

### 5.3 Expressive meaning and "descriptive ineffability"

The most precise formal home for *6-7* is the **expressivist** strand of semantics. **David Kaplan's** "The Meaning of 'Ouch' and 'Oops'" treats interjections as evidence for a semantics of "meaning as use," distinguishing *expressive* content (which displays a speaker's attitude or affective state) from *descriptive*, truth-apt content (Kaplan, 1999). **Christopher Potts** (2005, 2007) develops this into a systematic account of **expressives** (expletives, epithets, honorifics) as contributing non-truth-conditional, speaker-oriented "expressive meaning" — what Kaplan (1999) and later Gutzmann (2015) term "use-conditional" meaning — that is independent of a sentence's at-issue content.

Potts's diagnostic properties of expressives map onto *6-7* with uncanny precision. **Descriptive ineffability** — speakers asked to paraphrase an expressive cannot give a definition and instead resort to examples of appropriate use — is *exactly* the behavior of both teenagers and lexicographers confronted with *6-7*: they describe *when it is said*, not *what it denotes*. **Repeatability** — repetition strengthens an expressive rather than rendering it redundant — describes the chanted, echoic deployment of *6-7* in classrooms and arenas. That a dictionary's best account of the term is "impossible to define … but meaningful to the people who use it" is not lexicographic failure; it is the textbook signature of expressive (use-conditional) meaning.

### 5.4 Indexicality and third-wave sociolinguistics

If *6-7* is not denotational, what *is* its meaning? Sociolinguistic theory answers: **indexical**. **Michael Silverstein's** notion of *indexical order* holds that linguistic forms acquire social meaning by indexing macro-social categories and identities through micro-level interaction (Silverstein, 2003). **Penelope Eckert** (2008, 2018) argues that the meanings of linguistic variables are not fixed denotations but an **indexical field** — a term she introduced in "Variation and the Indexical Field" (2008) and developed in *Meaning and Linguistic Variation: The Third Wave in Sociolinguistics* (2018) — comprising ideologically linked potential meanings, activated in situated use to construct identity and signal stance. On this view, the "meaning" of *6-7* just *is* its indexing of Gen Alpha in-group membership, playful stance, and shared participation — a meaning that is real, learnable, and socially consequential, but categorically different from reference. Third-wave sociolinguistics thus supplies the positive account that truth-conditional semantics cannot: meaning as social-indexical, not denotational.

### 5.5 Slang as solidarity, and the anti-language

The sociology of slang independently predicts items like *6-7*. **Connie Eble's** *Slang and Sociability* (1996), built on more than 10,000 examples collected from university students, identifies the central purposes of slang as "cementing group identity and opposing authority," with slang "deliberately chosen … to mark … inclusion in, admiration for, or identification with a social group" (Eble, 1996, 2004). **M.A.K. Halliday's** concept of the **anti-language** (1976) describes how marginal or oppositional groups generate vocabulary that "serves to create and maintain social structure through conversation," often via **overlexicalization** — proliferating terms that signal in-group preoccupation rather than adding denotational distinctions. *6-7*, as a shibboleth that bonds adolescents and irritates authority figures, is slang performing its prototypical solidarity-and-opposition function, with the denotational content reduced essentially to zero.

### 5.6 Memetics and internet linguistics

Finally, the *mechanism* of *6-7*'s spread is memetic rather than semantic. **Richard Dawkins** coined "**meme**" in *The Selfish Gene* (1976) as "a unit of cultural transmission, or a unit of imitation" propagating by copying from mind to mind — by replication, not by conveying fixed content (Dawkins, 1976). **Gretchen McCulloch's** *Because Internet* (2019) shows that the "artfully disarrayed" form of animal-meme language made it "more likely to spread," attributing virality to sound, form, and play rather than semantic payload (McCulloch, 2019). *6-7* spreads as Dawkinsian imitation: a catchy form plus a gesture, replicating because it is "just funny to say" (Aleksic) — Shaq's participation-without-comprehension is the limiting case of meaning-free replication. The broader **"brainrot"** lexicon — *skibidi* (which Aleksic calls a "pseudo word," "completely meaningless but still … able to be pronounced," akin to "Scooby Doo" or "la la la"), *Ohio*, *gyat* — populates the same category, and Oxford's selection of "**brain rot**" as its 2024 Word of the Year (frequency up 230% year-on-year) marks the broader cultural recognition of deliberately contentless online language (Oxford, 2024; Today, 2024).

## 6. Discussion: What "6-7" Reveals About the Limits — and Scope — of Meaning-Based Theory

### 6.1 The lexicographic paradox is a category signal

The headline irony — a dictionary crowning a word it cannot define — dissolves once the term is correctly categorized. Dictionary.com's own gloss ("meaningless, ubiquitous, and nonsensical … still … meaningful to the people who use it because of the connection it fosters") is a near-paraphrase of Malinowski's phatic communion and of Potts's expressive, use-conditional meaning. Merriam-Webster's own slang-dictionary entry is equally telling: it defines *six seven* (interjection) as "a nonsensical expression connected to a song and a basketball player" (Merriam-Webster, *Slang Dictionary*) — that is, by *what it attaches to* rather than by what it denotes, exactly the "describe the use, not the sense" behavior that Potts's descriptive ineffability predicts. The institution of definition meets its limit not because *6-7* is sub-linguistic, but because the meaning-based, definitional model of the lexicon is *the wrong model for this class of item*. The paradox is diagnostic, not embarrassing.

### 6.2 Not a refutation but a scoping

It would overreach to say *6-7* "refutes" referential or truth-conditional semantics. Those theories were designed for the truth-apt, denotational core of the lexicon and remain powerful there. What *6-7* does is **make vivid the boundary** that the tradition itself acknowledges through its marginal treatment of interjections, expletives, and discourse markers. The honest conclusion is that meaning-based lexical theory is *locally adequate but globally incomplete*: it requires supplementation by (a) a use-based conception of meaning (Wittgenstein; construction grammar), (b) an expressive/use-conditional dimension (Kaplan; Potts), and (c) a social-indexical dimension (Silverstein; Eckert). *6-7* is valuable precisely because it is **phatic-only**: a near-pure specimen that isolates these dimensions from any confounding denotational content, in the way a controlled experiment isolates a variable.

### 6.3 A unifying claim

The convergence across literatures is the review's central finding. Anthropology (Malinowski), structural linguistics (Jakobson), philosophy of language (Wittgenstein, Kaplan, Potts), sociolinguistics (Silverstein, Eckert, Eble, Halliday), media studies (Miller), and internet linguistics (Dawkins, McCulloch) — developed independently and decades apart — all describe the same thing: language whose function is to establish contact, mark belonging, and perform affect rather than to denote. *6-7* is simply the most recent, most visible, and most analytically convenient instance. A complete theory of the lexicon must have a place for the phatic-only word; *6-7* shows what that place must look like.

### 6.4 Limitations and open questions

Several caveats temper these conclusions. First, the phenomenon is recent and the academic (as opposed to journalistic) literature on *6-7* specifically is thin; the analysis leans on expert commentary in popular outlets and on the application of established theory rather than on peer-reviewed studies of the term itself. Second, "meaninglessness" is partly a matter of degree and perspective: *6-7* arguably retains a faint indexical "meaning" (so-so, ambivalence) and certainly a robust social meaning, so claims that it means "nothing" should be read as "nothing *denotational*." Third, phatic-only terms are characteristically ephemeral; whether *6-7* lexicalizes a stable sense, fades, or leaves a constructional residue is an open empirical question well suited to longitudinal corpus study. Finally, some origin claims (e.g., the *10-67* police-code etymology) remain unverified speculation and should not be treated as established.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The "6-7" meme is a phatic-only slang term: an interjection whose communicative value is constituted almost entirely by social contact, in-group indexing, and affective performance, with denotational content bleached to near zero. Reviewed against meaning-based lexical theory, it does not collapse the referential and truth-conditional tradition so much as map that tradition's edge — the same edge marked by interjections, expletives, and discourse particles, which formal semantics has always handled by supplementary means. The most adequate accounts of *6-7* come from use-based (Wittgenstein, construction grammar), expressivist (Kaplan, Potts), and social-indexical (Silverstein, Eckert) frameworks, all of which permit "meaning" to be a conventionalized form-function pairing rather than a form-referent pairing. That a dictionary could name "impossible to define" as the defining feature of its Word of the Year is, read correctly, not a paradox but a precise empirical signal: the lexicon contains words whose meaning is their use, their stance, and their power to bond — and any theory that equates lexical meaning with reference will, at that boundary, run out of things to say exactly where *6-7* begins.
