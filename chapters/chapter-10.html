1. Why Network Security Matters [Spec 5.2.1]

Almost every organisation now stores valuable data on networked systems: customer records, payment details, medical histories, designs and trade secrets. A network connected to the internet can be attacked from anywhere in the world, at any time, by automated tools. Security failures are expensive in three distinct ways — and exam answers should be able to name all three.

ReasonExplanationExample Consequence
Data is valuableStolen personal and financial data can be sold or used for fraud; trade secrets benefit competitorsStolen card details used for fraudulent purchases; customer database sold on the dark web
Disruption is costlyDowntime stops sales and operations; recovery, investigation and ransom demands cost money; reputation damage drives customers awayAn online shop loses every sale while its site is down during a DoS attack
It is a legal dutyThe Data Protection Act 2018 requires organisations to keep personal data secure using appropriate measuresLarge fines from the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) after a preventable data breach
⚡ EXAM TIP

When asked 'why must this company secure its network?', cover more than one angle: financial loss, reputational damage AND the legal obligation under the Data Protection Act 2018. The legal point is the one students most often forget.

2. Social Engineering — Attacking the People [Spec 5.2.2]

Social engineering means manipulating people — rather than attacking machines — into giving away information or access. It works because humans trust, panic, and want to be helpful. No firewall can stop an employee who is tricked into handing over their password.

TechniqueHow It WorksExampleDefence
PhishingFake emails, texts or websites impersonating a trusted organisation trick the victim into revealing credentials or clicking a malicious link'Your bank account is locked — click here to verify your details' leading to a fake login pageCheck sender addresses and link destinations; never follow login links in emails; user training; spam filters
Pretexting (blagging)The attacker invents a convincing scenario (pretext) to persuade the victim to hand over information or perform an actionA phone caller claims to be 'IT support' and asks an employee for their password to 'fix an urgent fault'Verify identities through official channels; policies stating that staff NEVER ask for passwords
Shoulder surfingWatching someone enter a PIN, password or confidential data — in person or via CCTV/cameraReading a commuter's laptop screen on a train; watching a PIN at a cash machinePrivacy screens; shielding keypads; being aware of surroundings; screen-lock policies
💡 Why Humans Are the Weakest Link

Technical defences (firewalls, encryption, anti-malware) follow rules perfectly and never get tired. People, by contrast, can be rushed, frightened, distracted or flattered into breaking the rules — and one careless click can bypass thousands of pounds of security technology. That is why attackers increasingly target users instead of systems, and why staff training and clear policies are considered security measures just as much as software is.

2.1 Pharming and Eavesdropping [Spec 5.2.3]

AttackHow it works
PharmingThe victim is silently redirected from a genuine website to a fake copy (e.g. by tampering with DNS), where their login details are harvested. Unlike phishing, no mistaken click is needed.
EavesdroppingAn attacker intercepts data as it travels across a network — especially unencrypted traffic on public Wi-Fi. Defence: encryption (HTTPS, VPNs).
Unpatched softwareKnown security holes in out-of-date software are exploited. Defence: install updates promptly.
USB / digital devicesInfected USB sticks or devices plugged into a machine deliver malware or steal data. Defence: disable autorun, control use of removable media.

3. Technical Attacks: Hacking & Malware [Spec 5.2.3]

3.1 Unauthorised Access (Hacking)

Unauthorised access means gaining entry to a computer system, network or data without permission. Attackers may exploit weak passwords, unpatched software flaws, or stolen credentials. Unauthorised access is a criminal offence under the Computer Misuse Act 1990 (covered fully in Chapter 11).

3.2 Malware — Know All Six Types

Malware TypeHow It Spreads / OperatesWhat It Does
VirusAttaches itself to a host file or program; replicates when the infected file is opened or run; needs user action to spreadCorrupts or deletes files; spreads to other files and machines
WormSelf-replicates and spreads across networks by itself — needs NO host file and NO user actionConsumes bandwidth and resources; can carry destructive payloads; spreads extremely fast
TrojanDisguised as legitimate, desirable software (a game, a free utility); the USER installs it voluntarilyOpens a 'back door' for attackers; installs other malware; steals data. Does not self-replicate
RansomwareOften delivered by phishing or trojan; encrypts the victim's filesDemands payment (a ransom) for the decryption key; can paralyse whole organisations (e.g. hospitals)
SpywareInstalls silently, often bundled with other downloadsSecretly monitors the user's activity — sites visited, data entered — and sends it to the attacker
KeyloggerA form of spyware (software, or even a hardware device plugged in between keyboard and computer)Records every keystroke, capturing usernames, passwords and card numbers
⚡ EXAM TIP — Virus vs Worm vs Trojan

This three-way distinction is examined constantly. Virus = needs a HOST FILE and USER ACTION to spread. Worm = self-replicates across the NETWORK with no host file and no user action. Trojan = does not self-replicate at all; it relies on DISGUISE so the user installs it willingly. Use these exact discriminators in your answer.

3.3 Denial of Service (DoS)

A denial of service attack floods a server with a huge number of bogus requests so that it cannot respond to genuine users — the service becomes unusably slow or crashes entirely. The aim is disruption, not theft: an online retailer loses sales for every minute its site is down. (A distributed DoS — DDoS — uses many hijacked computers, a 'botnet', to generate the flood, making it far harder to block.)

3.4 SQL Injection

Many websites build database queries from what users type into input boxes. In an SQL injection attack, the attacker types fragments of SQL code into an input field (such as a login box). If the website inserts that input directly into its query, the attacker's code becomes part of the query — and can reveal, modify or delete data they should never be able to touch.

Expected query (user types "amir"):
  SELECT * FROM users WHERE name = 'amir'

Attacker types:   ' OR 1=1 --
Resulting query:
  SELECT * FROM users WHERE name = '' OR 1=1 --'

1=1 is ALWAYS true, so the query returns EVERY user record.
🛡️ Preventing SQL Injection

Input sanitisation: remove or neutralise special characters (such as quotes) from user input before it reaches the database.

Input validation: reject input that does not match the expected pattern, type or length — e.g. a username field should not accept ' OR 1=1 --.

The principle: never trust user input. Treat everything typed into a form as data, never as code.

4. Identifying Vulnerabilities [Spec 5.2.4]

Before defences can be improved, an organisation must find its weaknesses — ideally before an attacker does. The spec names five approaches.

MethodWhat It InvolvesWhat It Reveals
Penetration testingAuthorised specialists deliberately attempt to break into the system, simulating a real attack, then report what they found and how to fix itReal, exploitable weaknesses in technical defences AND in staff behaviour (e.g. who clicks a test phishing email)
Ethical (white-hat) hackingHacking skills used legally and with permission to improve security — penetration testers are ethical hackers; some companies run 'bug bounty' schemes rewarding reported flawsVulnerabilities found by people who think like attackers
Commercial analysis toolsAutomated software scans the network for known weaknesses: open ports, missing patches, weak passwords, misconfigured servicesLarge numbers of known technical vulnerabilities, quickly and repeatably
Reviewing network and user policiesChecking that security rules (password rules, access rights, acceptable use) exist, are up to date, and are actually followedProcedural weaknesses — e.g. ex-employees whose accounts were never disabled
Audit trailsAutomatic logs recording who did what and when: logins, file access, changes to settingsEvidence of suspicious activity; lets investigators trace how a breach happened and who was responsible
⚡ EXAM TIP

Penetration testing answers need the word authorised: it is testing carried out WITH the organisation's permission, to find weaknesses BEFORE real attackers do, with results REPORTED so they can be fixed. Without 'authorised', it's just a description of hacking.

5. Protecting Networks [Spec 5.2.5]

5.1 Authentication — Proving Who You Are

MethodHow It Works✅ Strengths⚠️ Weaknesses
PasswordsSecret string known only to the user; strong passwords are long and mix letters, digits and symbolsCheap; universal; easy to change if compromisedCan be guessed, cracked, phished, reused across sites, or written down
Two-factor authentication (2FA)Combines two different proof types — something you KNOW (password) plus something you HAVE (code sent to phone/app) or ARE (fingerprint)A stolen password alone is no longer enough to get inSlightly slower to log in; depends on access to the second factor
BiometricsMeasures a unique physical characteristic: fingerprint, face, irisCannot be forgotten or easily shared; very hard to fakeCannot be changed if compromised; scanner cost; false accept/reject rates; privacy concerns

5.2 Technical, Physical and Procedural Protections

MeasureWhat It DoesWhat It Protects Against
FirewallHardware or software that monitors incoming and outgoing traffic and blocks anything breaking its rules (e.g. traffic on suspicious ports)Unauthorised access from outside; some malware communication
Access control / user permissionsEach user is granted access ONLY to the files and systems their role requires (least privilege)Insider damage; limits how far an attacker with one stolen account can reach
Anti-malware softwareScans files and activity for known malware signatures and suspicious behaviour; quarantines and removes threats; must be kept updatedViruses, worms, trojans, ransomware, spyware, keyloggers
Encryption (stored and transmitted data)Scrambles data with an algorithm and key so it is unreadable without the key — applied to data at rest (disks, databases) and in transit (HTTPS)Stolen or intercepted data being read or used
Physical securityLocked server rooms, key cards, CCTV, security staff, locking equipment awayTheft of hardware; attackers plugging directly into the network; shoulder surfing
Backup and recoveryRegular copies of data kept securely (including off-site/offline); a tested plan to restore systemsRansomware (restore data without paying), hardware failure, fire, accidental deletion
Acceptable use policy (AUP)A signed document defining what users may and may not do: password rules, email/web use, personal devices, consequencesRisky user behaviour; gives the organisation grounds to act on misuse
Software updates / patchesInstalling security fixes promptly closes the known flaws that attackers and worms exploitAttacks that exploit unpatched, known vulnerabilities
⚠️ ENCRYPTION ≠ ACCESS PREVENTION

Encryption does not stop data being stolen — it makes stolen data unreadable without the key. If asked how encryption protects data, never say 'it stops hackers getting in'; say 'if the data is intercepted or stolen, it cannot be understood without the decryption key'.

5.1 Security and the Cloud [Spec 5.2.2]

Storing data in the cloud moves it onto someone else’s servers, reached over the internet. Consider: is data encrypted in transit and at rest? Who at the provider can access it? What happens if the provider is breached or goes offline? Strong passwords + two-factor authentication on cloud accounts are essential, because the login is now the only barrier.

5.2 Protecting Software at the Design Stage [Spec 5.2.5]

Security starts when software is written, not after: secure coding practices (e.g. validating every input), code reviews to spot vulnerabilities and bad programming practice, modular testing of each component, audit trails recording who did what, and keeping the operating system secured and patched.

6. Defence in Depth — Layered Protection [Spec 5.2.6]

No single measure is perfect — phishing slips past firewalls, passwords get stolen, software has undiscovered flaws. Defence in depth means using multiple overlapping layers, so an attacker who defeats one layer is stopped by the next. Six-mark scenario questions usually ask you to design exactly this.

LayerExample MeasuresStops
1. PhysicalLocked server room, key cards, CCTVTheft and direct tampering
2. PerimeterFirewall between LAN and internetUnauthorised external traffic
3. AuthenticationStrong passwords + 2FAStolen-credential logins
4. AuthorisationUser permissions / least privilegeOne compromised account reaching everything
5. Device & dataAnti-malware, patched software, encryptionMalware; reading of stolen data
6. People & processStaff training, acceptable use policy, audit trailsSocial engineering; undetected misuse
7. RecoveryOff-site backups and a tested recovery planPermanent loss from ransomware or disaster
🎯 Answer Framework for 'Design the Security' Scenario Questions

Step 1. Identify the threats the scenario implies (customer data → theft; web-facing → DoS, SQL injection; staff → phishing).

Step 2. Pick measures from different layers — at least one technical, one physical, one procedural/human.

Step 3. Link each measure to a threat: 'A firewall should be installed SO THAT external traffic that breaks the rules is blocked...'

Step 4. Mention backup/recovery — examiners reward recognising that prevention can fail.

7. Model Answers

Notice how each answer names the mechanism precisely, then develops it with a consequence — and how scenario answers tie every measure back to the situation.

✎ MODEL ANSWER

[4 marks]

Question: Describe how a phishing attack could be used to gain access to a company's network, and state TWO ways the company could reduce the risk. [4 marks]

The attacker sends employees an email that appears to come from a trusted source, such as the company's IT department (1). The email contains a link to a fake login page; when an employee enters their username and password, these credentials are captured by the attacker, who can then log in to the company network as that user (1). To reduce the risk, the company should train staff to check sender addresses and never enter credentials via emailed links (1), and enable two-factor authentication so that a stolen password alone is not enough to log in (1).

Why this scores full marks: ✓ Describes the mechanism (fake trusted email → fake page → credentials captured), not just 'a scam email' ✓ Explains how the attacker USES the stolen credentials — linking the attack to network access as the question asks ✓ Two distinct defences from different layers: human (training) and technical (2FA).

✎ MODEL ANSWER

[3 marks]

Question: Explain the difference between a virus and a worm. [3 marks]

A virus attaches itself to a host file or program and replicates only when a user opens or runs that infected file (1). A worm does not need a host file — it is a standalone program that self-replicates (1) and spreads across a network automatically, without any user action being required (1).

Why this scores full marks: ✓ Uses the two key discriminators: host file (virus needs one, worm doesn't) and user action (virus needs it, worm doesn't) ✓ Each statement is a clean, separate point ✓ Avoids vague phrasing like 'a worm is worse' — comparisons must be technical, not judgemental.

✎ MODEL ANSWER

[6 marks]

Question: An online retailer stores customers' personal and payment details on its networked systems. Discuss the measures the retailer should take to protect this data. [6 marks]

Because the retailer holds personal data, it has a legal duty under the Data Protection Act 2018 to keep it secure, as well as a commercial interest in protecting its reputation (1). A firewall should be placed between the company network and the internet to block unauthorised incoming traffic (1). All customer data should be encrypted, both when stored and when transmitted using HTTPS, so that even if data is intercepted or stolen it cannot be read without the key (1). Staff accounts should use strong passwords with two-factor authentication, and access controls should ensure employees can only reach the data their role requires (1). Because the website takes typed input, all input fields must be validated and sanitised to prevent SQL injection attacks on the customer database (1). Finally, the retailer should keep software patched, train staff to recognise phishing, and take regular off-site backups so that data can be recovered after ransomware or hardware failure (1).

Why this scores full marks: ✓ Opens with WHY protection is needed, including the legal duty — sets up a discussion, not a list ✓ Covers multiple layers: perimeter (firewall), data (encryption), identity (2FA/access control), application (input sanitisation), human (training) and recovery (backups) ✓ Every measure is justified with 'so that...' reasoning ✓ Measures are matched to this scenario (web-facing retailer with payment data), not generic.

8. Common Mistakes

✗ COMMON MISTAKES — DON'T DO THESE!

✗ Calling every attack 'hacking' — use the precise term: phishing, pretexting, DoS, SQL injection, ransomware. Vague answers score vaguely.

✗ Mixing up virus, worm and trojan — virus needs a host file and user action; worm self-replicates over the network with neither; trojan relies on disguise and does not self-replicate.

✗ Saying a firewall 'stops all viruses' — a firewall filters network traffic by rules; it is not anti-malware software. Don't merge the two in one answer.

✗ Saying encryption 'stops hackers getting in' — encryption makes stolen or intercepted data unreadable without the key; it does not prevent access itself.

✗ Describing penetration testing without the word 'authorised' — unauthorised probing is a crime under the Computer Misuse Act 1990, not a security measure.

✗ Forgetting the human layer in scenario answers — staff training and acceptable use policies are valid, creditworthy security measures. Most real breaches start with a person.

✗ Treating a DoS attack as data theft — DoS makes a service UNAVAILABLE by flooding it with requests; nothing is necessarily stolen. Its cost is downtime and lost business.

✗ Saying SQL injection is 'hacking the website' — be precise: malicious SQL entered into an input field becomes part of the database query; it is prevented by input sanitisation and validation.

10. Quick Reference — Key Terms & Revision

★ QUICK REFERENCE — KEY TERMS

Social engineering: Manipulating people (not machines) into revealing information or granting access

Phishing: Fake emails/messages impersonating trusted organisations to steal credentials or deliver malware

Pretexting (blagging): Inventing a convincing scenario to trick a victim into handing over information

Shoulder surfing: Watching someone enter a PIN, password or confidential data

Unauthorised access (hacking): Entering a system or data without permission — illegal under the Computer Misuse Act 1990

Virus: Malware that attaches to host files and replicates when they are opened/run

Worm: Standalone malware that self-replicates across networks without user action

Trojan: Malware disguised as legitimate software so the user installs it; does not self-replicate

Ransomware: Encrypts the victim's files and demands payment for the decryption key

Spyware: Secretly monitors the user's activity and reports it to the attacker

Keylogger: Records every keystroke to capture passwords and card details

DoS attack: Floods a server with bogus requests so genuine users cannot use the service

SQL injection: Malicious SQL entered into input fields to manipulate a database; prevented by input sanitisation/validation

Penetration testing: Authorised simulated attacks to find weaknesses before real attackers do

Ethical hacking: Hacking skills used legally, with permission, to improve security

Audit trail: Automatic log of who did what and when — used to detect and investigate misuse

Authentication: Verifying identity: passwords, two-factor authentication, biometrics

Access control: Granting each user only the permissions their role requires (least privilege)

Firewall: Monitors and filters network traffic according to rules

Anti-malware: Scans for, quarantines and removes malicious software; needs regular updates

Encryption: Scrambling stored or transmitted data so it is unreadable without the key

Acceptable use policy: A document defining what network users may and may not do

Defence in depth: Multiple overlapping layers of protection so no single failure exposes the data
