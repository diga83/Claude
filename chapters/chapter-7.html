1. Risks to Data and Personal Information [Spec 3.1.1]

📋 SPEC 3.1.1

Be aware of risks: unauthorised access, deliberate damage by malware, accidental deletion, phishing, pharming.

1. Unauthorised Access Gaining entry to systems without permission — via stolen passwords, brute force or exploiting vulnerabilities2. Deliberate Damage by Malware Malicious software (viruses, ransomware, spyware, worms) intentionally corrupts, encrypts or steals data
3. Accidental Deletion Data lost unintentionally by users — files deleted, overwritten or formatted without backup4. Phishing Fraudulent emails/messages impersonate trusted organisations to steal login credentials or financial data
5. Pharming DNS manipulation silently redirects users to fake websites without any deceptive message being needed6. Identity Theft Personal data stolen and used to fraudulently open accounts, make purchases or commit crimes in the victim's name
💡 Why Personal Data Is Valuable to Attackers

Financial gain: Bank account details, credit card numbers, PayPal credentials can be used directly for fraud.

Identity theft: Name, date of birth, address, NI number — enough to open bank accounts or loans in someone's name.

Blackmail: Private photos, messages or embarrassing information can be used to extort victims.

Corporate espionage: Trade secrets, client lists and financial projections stolen to gain competitive advantage.

Selling data: Personal data (email addresses, preferences) sold on the dark web to spammers and fraudsters.

⚡ EXAM TIP

Questions about security methods often give a specific scenario and ask 'describe TWO methods to protect against this threat'. Always: (1) Name the exact method, (2) Explain how the method works mechanically, (3) Link it to the threat in the scenario. 'Use a firewall' = 0 marks. 'A firewall monitors all incoming network traffic and blocks any packets that do not match the permitted rules — this would prevent the attacker from establishing a connection to the server' = 2 marks.

3. Online Payment Systems [Spec 3.1.3]

📋 SPEC 3.1.3

Know about online payment systems, third-party payment systems, bank cards, contactless payment using NFC and how payments are protected.

Payment MethodHow It WorksSecurity FeaturesWhen Used
Bank Card (Chip & PIN)EMV chip generates unique transaction code; PIN verified locallyChip = encrypted; PIN = second factor; bank verifies merchantIn-person retail; restaurants; fuel stations
Contactless Card (NFC)Near-field radio communication tap (under 4cm); below limit (~£100) no PINShort range (4cm) makes interception very hard; transaction limit; bank monitoringLow-value quick purchases; transport; vending machines

📌 How Online Payments Are Protected — Key Points

HTTPS / TLS encryption: All payment page data is encrypted in transit — card numbers cannot be intercepted.

3D Secure / Verified by Visa: Additional authentication step (OTP to phone or biometric) before online payment completes.

CVV (Card Verification Value): Three-digit number on back of card — not stored by merchants; proves physical card possession.

Tokenisation: Apple/Google Pay replace real card number with a one-time token — merchant never sees actual card details.

Bank fraud monitoring: Banks use AI to detect unusual spending patterns and block suspicious transactions automatically.

NFC short range: Contactless payments only work within 4cm — very difficult to intercept in real-world conditions.

4.1 How to Stay Safe Online [Spec 3.2.5]

1. Use strong, unique passwords for every account — never reuse passwords Use a password manager; 12+ characters; mix letters, numbers and symbols2. Enable Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) on all important accounts Email, banking, social media — even if password stolen, MFA blocks access
3. Keep devices, operating systems and apps updated Security patches close vulnerabilities; automatic updates are safest4. Be sceptical of emails, links and attachments Check sender address; hover over links; never enter passwords from email links
5. Only use HTTPS websites for sensitive activities Look for padlock icon; never submit payment on HTTP sites6. Use a VPN on public Wi-Fi networks Cafés, hotels, airports — public Wi-Fi is easily intercepted without VPN
7. Review and restrict privacy settings on social media Limit who can see posts; never share location publicly; audit friend/follower lists8. Understand what personal information you share online Date of birth, address, school name are used by identity thieves and social engineers
⚡ EXAM TIP

Impact questions often ask for 'ONE positive and ONE negative effect' — always state the effect clearly then explain WHY it is positive/negative with a real example. Don't just say 'people can work from home'; say 'people can work from home which saves them commuting time and costs, improving work-life balance, but this can also blur the boundary between work and personal time, leading to stress.'

📌 Causes and Consequences of the Digital Divide

Causes of unequal access: Cost of devices and broadband; lack of infrastructure (rural/developing); limited electricity; language barriers; age; disability; digital literacy gaps.

Local inequality: Rural UK village with ADSL 2 Mbps vs London with FTTP 1 Gbps — students in rural areas disadvantaged for homework, video calls, online resources.

Global inequality: Sub-Saharan Africa 22% internet penetration vs North America 95% — restricts economic development, education, healthcare access.

Consequences of no access: Cannot apply for jobs online; cannot access government services; excluded from online banking; cannot participate in e-learning; socially isolated.

Solutions: Government-subsidised broadband; device lending schemes; digital skills training in libraries; satellite internet (Starlink) for remote areas.

8. Legal Implications of Digital Technology [Spec 3.8.1–3.8.3]

8.1 Data Protection Law [Spec 3.8.1]

📋 SPEC 3.8.1

Know about data protection, the legal requirements of those storing data about individuals and an individual's legal rights.

Data protection laws (such as the UK Data Protection Act 2018 and GDPR) set out rules for how personal data must be handled and give individuals rights over their own data.

Data Protection PrincipleWhat It Means for OrganisationsIndividual's Rights
Data must be used fairly and lawfullyOrganisations must have a legal basis for collecting and processing personal data — cannot collect 'just in case'Individuals must give informed consent for data use; can withdraw consent
Data must be used for specified, explicit purposesData collected for one purpose (e.g. delivery address) cannot be used for another (e.g. marketing) without consentRight to know exactly what their data will be used for before providing it
Data must be adequate, relevant and not excessiveOnly collect data actually needed for the purpose — not everything about a personRight to not have unnecessary personal data collected and stored
Data must be accurate and kept up to dateOrganisations must correct inaccurate data when identified; outdated data should be updatedRight to rectification — can request correction of inaccurate personal data
Data must not be kept longer than necessaryData deleted when no longer needed for its original purpose; retention policies requiredRight to erasure ('right to be forgotten') — can request deletion of personal data
Data must be kept secureAppropriate technical and organisational security measures must be in place to protect personal dataRight to compensation if data breach caused by poor security leads to harm
Data must not be transferred outside the region without protectionData about EU/UK citizens cannot be sent to countries without equivalent data protection lawsRights follow the data — even if stored in USA, EU laws still protect EU citizens' data
📋 What Counts as 'Personal Data'?

Name, address, date of birth, email address, phone number

IP address, device ID, location data, cookies (can identify a person)

Health records, financial information, employment records

Photographs, CCTV footage, voice recordings (if they identify a person)

Sensitive personal data (extra protection): Race, religion, political opinions, sexual orientation, biometric data, criminal record

8.2 Copyright Legislation [Spec 3.8.2]

📋 SPEC 3.8.2

Understand how copyright legislation affects the use of digital information and media.

Copyright protects creators' original works automatically from the moment of creation. In the digital age, copyright infringement is extremely common because copying is so easy — but it is still illegal.

Copyright IssueWhat the Law SaysWhat Users CAN DoWhat Users CANNOT Do
Music and audioProtected by copyright from the moment it is created; lasts 70 years after creator's deathStream on licensed platforms (Spotify); buy digital downloads; listen to radioDownload illegally (piracy); share MP3 files; use in videos without licence
Images and photographsPhotographer automatically owns copyright; stock photo sites license useUse Creative Commons-licensed images with attribution; buy stock licensesCopy images from Google without a licence; use in commercial work without permission
SoftwareProtected under copyright; licence defines permitted useUse software within licence terms; install on permitted number of devicesCopy and share software; crack serial keys; modify without permission
Written contentAuthor automatically owns copyright; applies to web content tooQuote short extracts for criticism/review with attributionCopy entire articles; reproduce without permission; pass off as own work (plagiarism)
Video contentCreator owns copyright; platform licences matter (YouTube, Netflix)Watch on licensed platform; share links; create transformative commentaryDownload and share Netflix content; upload copyright films to YouTube

⚠️ Common Copyright Misconceptions

'It's free online so I can use it': WRONG — online content is still copyrighted unless explicitly licensed for free use (e.g. Creative Commons).

'I'll credit the source so it's fine': WRONG — attribution does not grant permission to use copyrighted material. You need a licence.

'I'm not making money from it': WRONG — copyright applies regardless of whether you profit. Non-commercial use still requires permission.

'The company is huge, they won't notice': Legally irrelevant. Copyright infringement is infringement regardless of the copyright owner's size.

LEGALLY CORRECT: Always check the licence (Creative Commons, CC0, commercial licence). If unsure — ask or don't use.

9. Health & Safety Issues from ICT Use [Spec 3.8.6]

📋 SPEC 3.8.6

Understand the health and safety issues that arise from individuals' use of ICT and know how they can be minimised.

Using ICT equipment for extended periods can cause a range of physical and psychological health issues. The spec requires you to know what these are AND how they can be minimised.

RSI (Repetitive Strain Injury) Pain, numbness and weakness in hands, wrists, arms and shoulders from repetitive keyboard or mouse use Symptoms: Aching wrists; tingling fingers; weakness in grip; pain worse after use Solutions: Ergonomic keyboard and mouse; regular breaks; wrist rests; stretching exercisesEye Strain / CVS Computer Vision Syndrome — tired, sore, dry or blurry eyes from prolonged screen use without adequate breaks Symptoms: Headaches; blurred vision; dry eyes; difficulty focusing; light sensitivity Solutions: 20-20-20 rule (every 20 min look 20 feet away for 20 sec); adjust brightness; anti-glare screenBack and Neck Pain Poor posture while sitting at a desk leads to muscle strain, disc problems and chronic pain Symptoms: Lower back pain; stiff neck; shoulder tension; headaches from tension Solutions: Adjustable chair at correct height; monitor at eye level; lumbar support; take standing breaks
Psychological Effects Mental health impacts from excessive or problematic internet use — addiction, anxiety, cyberbullying, social pressure Symptoms: Anxiety when without phone; poor sleep; low self-esteem from comparison; social isolation Solutions: Set screen time limits; digital detox periods; school cyberbullying policies; parental monitoringHearing Damage Listening to loud audio through earphones for extended periods causes gradual, permanent hearing loss Symptoms: Ringing in ears (tinnitus); difficulty following conversations; permanent hearing reduction Solutions: Maximum volume limits on devices; noise-cancelling headphones at lower volume; 60/60 rule (60% volume 60 min max)Sedentary Lifestyle Prolonged sitting with insufficient physical movement increases risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease and diabetes Symptoms: Weight gain; reduced fitness; fatigue; poor circulation Solutions: Standing desk; scheduled walking breaks; lunch-time exercise; active commuting alternatives

📋 Employer Responsibilities for ICT Health and Safety

DSE Regulations: Display Screen Equipment regulations require employers to: assess workstations; provide suitable equipment; offer eye tests; provide training on safe use.

Regular breaks: Employees should take short breaks from screen work every hour — stand, stretch, move around.

Risk assessment: Employers must identify and reduce health risks from ICT use in the workplace.

Equipment provision: Ergonomic chairs, keyboard, mouse and wrist rests should be provided where needed.

Eye tests: Employers must offer to pay for eye tests for employees who regularly use display screen equipment.

✅ Individual Actions to Reduce ICT Environmental Impact

✓ Keep devices longer — extend the lifecycle rather than buying new every 2 years

✓ Buy refurbished or second-hand devices where possible

✓ Use energy saving mode; switch off rather than leaving on standby

✓ Recycle properly — use manufacturer take-back schemes or authorised e-waste recyclers

✓ Choose cloud providers that use renewable energy (Google, Microsoft committed to 100% renewable)

✓ Stream at lower quality when possible — 4K streaming uses 3x more data (and server energy) than HD

✓ Donate or sell working devices rather than discarding them

11. Model Answers

Each answer shows the complete structure: positive AND negative (when required); always linked to a specific context; developed with 'because' and 'which means'.

✎ MODEL ANSWER

[4 marks]

Q: Describe ONE positive and ONE negative social impact of the internet on individuals. [4 marks]

Positive: The internet gives individuals better access to information and new ways of learning (1). For example, a student in a rural area can access online courses, lecture videos and digital libraries that they would otherwise have no access to — equalising educational opportunity regardless of location (1). Negative: The internet has contributed to reduced physical activity for many people (1). Activities that previously required going outside — such as shopping, gaming, social interaction and entertainment — can now all be done from a screen, leading to a more sedentary lifestyle with associated health risks such as obesity and cardiovascular disease (1).

Why this scores full marks: ✓ Two distinct impacts — one positive, one negative — each earned 2 marks ✓ Positive: 'access to information' developed with rural student example — shows the specific mechanism ✓ Negative: 'reduced physical activity' developed with 'activities done from screen' and health consequence ✓ Both answers developed beyond the basic point — the second mark requires explanation of WHY or WHAT CONSEQUENCE

✎ MODEL ANSWER

[4 marks]

Q: Explain TWO benefits of working from home for an employee. [4 marks]

1. The employee saves time and money by not commuting to work (1). For a person who commutes one hour each way, working from home saves two hours every day — this time can be used for personal activities, exercise or rest, improving work-life balance (1). 2. The employee has greater flexibility over their working hours and environment (1). They can adjust their schedule to meet personal commitments such as school drop-offs, and can personalise their workspace with better equipment or a quieter setting that suits their working style — which can improve productivity (1).

Why this scores full marks: ✓ TWO distinct benefits — not 'flexibility' expressed two different ways ✓ Benefit 1: no commuting → specific quantification (2 hours saved) → consequence (work-life balance) ✓ Benefit 2: flexibility → specific example (school drop-off) → consequence (productivity) ✓ Each benefit earns 2 marks: the benefit (1) + the developed explanation or consequence (1)

✎ MODEL ANSWER

[4 marks]

Q: Explain what is meant by the 'digital divide' and describe ONE consequence for individuals who have limited internet access. [4 marks]

The digital divide refers to the gap between those who have access to the internet and digital technology and those who do not (1). This inequality exists both within countries — for example between urban and rural areas — and globally between developed and developing nations (1). One consequence for individuals with limited access is that they are excluded from online government services (1). For example, in countries where benefits applications, tax returns and medical appointments must be completed online, those without internet access cannot participate — they are further disadvantaged compared to citizens who can complete these tasks from home (1).

Why this scores full marks: ✓ Definition of digital divide: gap between those with and without access — specifying both within and between countries ✓ Consequence: specific example (government services) developed with concrete impact (benefits, tax, appointments) ✓ Answer demonstrates understanding of the inequality — not just 'they can't use the internet' ✓ Four marks: definition (1) + dimension of inequality (1) + consequence named (1) + consequence developed (1)

12. Common Mistakes

✗ COMMON MISTAKES

✗ Saying 'cyberbullying' without explaining what it is — always define: 'cyberbullying is the use of digital technology to repeatedly bully, threaten or embarrass another person, typically through social media, messaging or online gaming.'

✗ Giving only positive OR only negative impacts when asked to 'describe the impact' — most impact questions need BOTH sides unless the question says 'one positive impact'.

✗ Confusing data protection with cyber security — data protection is a LEGAL framework about how personal data is collected and used. Cyber security is about protecting systems from attack. They are related but different.

✗ Saying copyright means you can't copy anything — the law allows fair use for commentary, criticism, education and parody. The key is whether you have a licence or whether the use falls within a legal exception.

✗ Writing 'the internet causes less social interaction' without explaining HOW — always develop: 'people spend time on social media instead of meeting face to face, which reduces the depth and quality of real relationships.'

✗ Confusing 'information rich' with 'wealthy' — the digital divide is about ACCESS to information and technology, not financial wealth (though they are correlated). A wealthy person without internet is 'information poor' in this context.

✗ Describing RSI as 'back pain' — RSI (Repetitive Strain Injury) affects HANDS, WRISTS and ARMS from repetitive keyboard/mouse use. Back pain is a separate issue from poor posture and seating.

✗ Not stating individual rights when discussing data protection — always mention: right to access their data, right to correct inaccurate data, right to erasure ('be forgotten'), right to know how data is used.

✗ Saying WFH is always better — for some people (carers, introverts, long commuters) it is excellent; for others (social workers, manual workers, those with small homes or young children) it is very difficult. Balanced answers score better.

✗ Forgetting environmental impacts of ICT — e-waste, data centre energy consumption and rare earth mining are all in the spec. These must be learned alongside the benefits of going digital.

14. Quick Reference — Key Terms & Revision

★ KEY TERMS — QUICK REFERENCE

Unauthorised Access: Gaining entry to a computer system or data without the owner's permission

Malware: Malicious software designed to damage, disrupt or gain unauthorised access to systems

Phishing: A social engineering attack using fraudulent messages to trick users into revealing credentials or clicking malicious links

Pharming: Technical attack that silently redirects users from legitimate websites to fake ones via DNS manipulation — no user mistake required

Identity Theft: Stealing personal data to fraudulently impersonate someone for financial gain or other crimes

CAPTCHA: Challenge-response test to distinguish humans from bots on login forms

Biometrics: Authentication using unique biological characteristics: fingerprint, face, iris, voice

Data Protection Law: Legal framework (e.g. GDPR/DPA) governing how organisations collect, store, use and protect personal data

Right to Erasure: Individual's legal right under data protection law to request deletion of their personal data

Copyright: Legal protection automatically given to creators of original works (music, writing, software, images)

Digital Divide: The gap between those with good internet access and digital skills and those without — locally and globally

Information Rich: Individuals/communities with full internet access, digital skills and devices — benefiting from digital opportunities

Information Poor: Individuals/communities without reliable internet, digital devices or skills — excluded from digital benefits

Working from Home (WFH): Using internet and digital devices to perform employment duties from home rather than a central workplace

RSI (Repetitive Strain Injury): Pain and damage in hands, wrists or arms caused by repetitive keyboard or mouse use

Computer Vision Syndrome (CVS): Eye strain, headaches and blurred vision from prolonged screen use — also called digital eye strain

Ergonomics: The science of designing workspaces and equipment to minimise injury and maximise comfort and efficiency

E-waste: Discarded electronic devices — a growing environmental problem due to toxic materials and poor recycling

WEEE Directive: EU/UK legislation requiring manufacturers to take back and properly recycle electronic waste

Carbon Footprint: The total greenhouse gas emissions (CO2 equivalent) produced by a person, organisation or activity

Surveillance: Monitoring of individuals' activities, movements or communications — by governments, employers or corporations

Monitoring: Tracking and recording user activity (web browsing, email, location) — may be for security, advertising or control

Individuals (-): Job losses; skills gap; cyberbullying; social isolation; reduced activity; privacy invasion

Organisations (-): Security threats; greater competition; data compliance costs; downtime risk

Society (-): Digital divide; inequality; surveillance; changing social norms; misinformation
