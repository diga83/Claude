1. Environmental Impact: Manufacture, Use & Disposal [Spec 5.1]

Every digital device has an environmental cost across its whole lifecycle. The exam expects you to discuss manufacture, use and disposal as three distinct stages — and to balance the negatives against the ways technology can help the environment.

1.1 Manufacture

IssueExplanation
Raw and rare-earth materialsDevices need metals such as gold, copper, lithium, cobalt and rare-earth elements. Mining them damages landscapes, pollutes water and, in some regions, involves poor or dangerous working conditions
EnergyManufacturing chips and screens is extremely energy-intensive — for many devices, more energy is used making them than they will ever consume in use
WaterSemiconductor fabrication uses millions of litres of ultra-pure water, straining local supplies
Factory conditions and transportLong global supply chains add shipping emissions; assembly work raises labour-condition concerns

1.2 Use

IssueExplanation
Electricity consumptionBillions of devices charging and running consume vast amounts of electricity — much still generated from fossil fuels, contributing to carbon emissions

Streaming and 'invisible' energyStreaming video feels free but every hour watched consumes energy in servers, networks and devices — the cost is hidden from the user

1.4 Technology Helping the Environment

♻️ Reduce · Reuse · Recycle

Reduce: keep devices longer; buy repairable models; choose energy-efficient hardware and settings.

Reuse: refurbish and resell old devices; donate working equipment to schools and charities.

Recycle: dispose of e-waste through proper schemes so metals are recovered and toxins handled safely — never in household bins.

2. Privacy, Data Collection & Surveillance [Spec 5.2]

Modern technology collects personal data on an enormous scale: what we search, buy, watch, say to voice assistants — and where we are, minute by minute. The ethical question the exam loves: where is the line between useful/safe and intrusive?

IssueThe Case FOR (benefits)The Case AGAINST (concerns)
Personal data collectionPersonalised services and recommendations; free services funded by advertising; better products built from usage dataUsers rarely understand what is collected; data sold or shared with third parties; profiles built without meaningful consent; breaches expose data forever
Location trackingNavigation; finding lost phones; emergency services locating callers; parents reassured about childrenConstant record of everywhere a person goes; reveals home, workplace, beliefs (e.g. places of worship); can enable stalking or misuse by apps
Surveillance (CCTV, online monitoring)Deters and helps solve crime; protects public spaces; monitoring can detect terrorism and abuseErodes privacy of innocent people; facial recognition can misidentify; 'chilling effect' on free expression; who watches the watchers?
⚡ EXAM TIP — The Security vs Privacy Debate

Surveillance questions are classic 'discuss' material. Strong answers weigh collective security ('CCTV evidence helps convict offenders, making public spaces safer') against individual privacy ('innocent people are recorded constantly without consent'), then conclude with a position — for example, that surveillance is acceptable when it is proportionate, targeted and legally regulated.

3. Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning & Bias [Spec 5.3]

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the broad field of making computers perform tasks that normally need human intelligence. Machine learning (ML) is the main modern approach: instead of being explicitly programmed with rules, the system learns patterns from training data. That single fact drives every ethical issue in this section.

3.1 Algorithmic Bias

⚠️ Biased Data In → Biased Decisions Out

An ML system is only as fair as the data it learned from. If the training data under-represents a group or contains historical prejudice, the system's decisions will be systematically unfair — at scale, automatically, with a false appearance of objectivity.

Example 1 — facial recognition: systems trained mostly on lighter-skinned faces have shown markedly higher error rates for darker-skinned faces — serious when used by police or for unlocking services.

Example 2 — recruitment: a CV-screening tool trained on a company's past hires (mostly men) learned to downgrade applications associated with women.

Example 3 — loans/insurance: models trained on historical lending data can repeat past discrimination against certain postcodes or groups.

3.2 Accountability and Autonomous Vehicles

QuestionWhy It Is Difficult
Who is accountable when AI makes a harmful decision?The programmer? The company deploying it? The provider of the training data? The user? Complex ML models can be 'black boxes' whose individual decisions are hard even for their creators to explain
Autonomous vehicles — unavoidable accidentsIf a crash is unavoidable, how should the car be programmed to choose between outcomes (protect its passenger vs pedestrians)? Who decides those rules, and who is liable — the 'driver', the manufacturer or the software developer?
Should AI make high-stakes decisions at all?AI now influences medical diagnoses, sentencing recommendations and benefit decisions. Many argue a human must remain responsible for the final decision ('human in the loop')

3.3 The Digital Divide

The digital divide is the gap between those who have access to modern digital technology (devices, fast internet, digital skills) and those who do not. It exists between countries and within them — affecting rural areas, low-income households and older people. As services (banking, shopping, government, education) move online, those on the wrong side of the divide are increasingly disadvantaged — a problem made vivid when schooling moved online during the pandemic and some students had no device or broadband to learn with.

4. The Law: DPA 2018, Computer Misuse Act & Copyright [Spec 5.4]

You must know three UK laws: what each covers, and which law applies to a given scenario. Learn the names and dates precisely.

4.1 Data Protection Act 2018 (implementing GDPR)

Controls how organisations collect, store and process personal data — any data about an identifiable living person. Enforced by the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), which can issue substantial fines.

4.2 Computer Misuse Act 1990

The UK's anti-hacking law. Learn its three main offences in order of seriousness:

OffenceWhat It CoversExample
1. Unauthorised access to computer materialAccessing programs or data without permission — even just lookingGuessing a friend's password and reading their messages
2. Unauthorised access with intent to commit a further offenceHacking in as a step towards another crime (fraud, theft, blackmail)Breaking into a bank system intending to transfer money out
3. Unauthorised modification of computer materialChanging or deleting data/programs without permission — including creating and spreading malwareReleasing ransomware; deleting a company's files; defacing a website

4.3 Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988

Protects original creative works — including program code, music, images, films, games and written text — from being copied, distributed or sold without the copyright holder's permission. Copyright is automatic: the creator does not need to register it.

⚡ EXAM TIP — Which Law?

Match the scenario keyword to the law: personal data mishandled/leaked → Data Protection Act 2018. Hacking, unauthorised access, malware → Computer Misuse Act 1990. Copying software, music, images, code → Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988. Always give the full name AND the year.

5. Intellectual Property & Software Licensing [Spec 5.5]

Intellectual property (IP) means creations of the mind — code, designs, music, inventions, brand names — that the creator owns and controls. A software licence is the legal agreement setting out what a user may do with a program. The two licensing models you must compare:

FeatureProprietary (closed-source)Open-source
DefinitionSource code kept secret; users buy a licence to USE the software onlySource code made freely available; anyone may view, modify and redistribute it under the licence terms
ExamplesMicrosoft Windows, Microsoft Office, Adobe PhotoshopLinux, Python, LibreOffice, Firefox, GIMP
CostUsually paid (one-off or subscription)Usually free of charge
ModificationNot allowed — code is hidden and legally protectedAllowed and encouraged — software can be customised to exact needs
SupportProfessional support from the vendor; guaranteed updates while supportedCommunity forums and documentation; paid support exists for major projects but is not guaranteed
Quality & security argumentDedicated paid developers; code secrecy hides flaws from attackers (but also from reviewers)'Many eyes' — anyone can inspect the code, so bugs and security flaws can be found and fixed quickly
DrawbacksCost; locked to vendor's decisions ('vendor lock-in'); cannot fix or extend it yourselfVariable documentation; usability can lag; no contractual guarantee anyone will fix your problem

6. How to Answer 6-Mark 'Discuss' Questions [Spec 5.6]

Topic 5 is where extended-response questions live. They are marked with levels: the best answers make points on both sides, develop each point with consequences, use technical vocabulary, and finish with a justified conclusion.

🎯 The D-E-B-A-T-E Structure

Step 1. Define any key term in the question (one sentence).

Step 2. Side A: two developed points FOR — each with a 'because...' and a 'which means...'.

Step 3. Side B: two developed points AGAINST, equally developed.

Step 4. Conclusion: take a position and justify it — 'Overall, X outweighs Y because...'. Never end with 'it depends' and nothing more.

Common failure: a one-sided list of undeveloped points caps the answer at the bottom level no matter how many points are made.

7. Model Answers

Note how the 6-mark answer balances both sides and commits to a conclusion, and how legal answers name the exact law and year.

✎ MODEL ANSWER

[3 marks]

Question: Describe the three main offences under the Computer Misuse Act 1990. [3 marks]

The first offence is unauthorised access to computer material — accessing programs or data without permission, such as logging into someone's account using a guessed password (1). The second is unauthorised access with intent to commit a further offence — for example, hacking into a system in order to commit fraud or theft (1). The third is unauthorised modification of computer material — changing or deleting data or programs without permission, which includes creating and distributing malware such as viruses or ransomware (1).

Why this scores full marks: ✓ All three offences named accurately and in order of seriousness ✓ Each offence is illustrated with a concrete example ✓ Correctly identifies that spreading malware falls under unauthorised modification — a detail examiners specifically look for.

✎ MODEL ANSWER

[4 marks]

Question: Explain how algorithmic bias can occur in a machine learning system used to shortlist job applicants. [4 marks]

A machine learning system learns patterns from training data rather than being explicitly programmed (1). If the training data consists of the company's past hiring decisions, and those decisions favoured one group — for example, mostly male employees — the system learns to associate that group's characteristics with 'good candidates' (1). As a result, it systematically downgrades applications from under-represented groups, even though no programmer intended this (1). Because the system processes every application automatically and appears objective, the unfair decisions are applied at scale and may go unnoticed without testing for bias (1).

Why this scores full marks: ✓ Starts with the mechanism — ML learns from data — which is the root cause ✓ Traces the chain: biased historical data → learned association → unfair output ✓ Adds the 'scale and false objectivity' point, showing why algorithmic bias is more dangerous than one biased human ✓ Stays within the scenario (job applicants) throughout.

✎ MODEL ANSWER

[6 marks]

Question: A city council plans to install facial-recognition CCTV throughout the city centre. Discuss the benefits and drawbacks of this plan. [6 marks]

One benefit is improved security: facial recognition can identify known offenders in real time, allowing police to respond quickly, and recorded footage provides evidence that helps convict criminals — which may also deter crime in the first place. A second benefit is efficiency: automated monitoring covers far more cameras than human operators could watch, so incidents are less likely to be missed. However, a major drawback is the loss of privacy: every innocent person in the city centre is scanned and potentially tracked without giving consent, creating a detailed record of their movements. A further drawback is algorithmic bias and error: facial-recognition systems have shown higher misidentification rates for some ethnic groups, so innocent people could be wrongly stopped, and such errors undermine public trust. There are also data protection concerns — the council must store and secure highly sensitive biometric data in line with the Data Protection Act 2018. Overall, while the security benefits are real, the plan is only justifiable if it is proportionate and tightly regulated — for example, used to search for specific suspects rather than to track everyone — because permanent mass surveillance of innocent people is too high a price for a general improvement in security.

Why this scores full marks: ✓ Two developed benefits AND two developed drawbacks — genuinely balanced ✓ Uses precise vocabulary: facial recognition, algorithmic bias, biometric data, proportionate ✓ Brings in relevant law (DPA 2018) — cross-topic links earn the top level ✓ Conclusion takes a clear, justified position rather than sitting on the fence.

8. Common Mistakes

✗ COMMON MISTAKES — DON'T DO THESE!

✗ Citing the wrong law for the scenario — hacking is NOT a Data Protection Act issue (Computer Misuse Act 1990), and copying software is NOT a Computer Misuse issue (Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988).

✗ Writing 'Data Protection Act' without the year, or 'the GDPR Act' — the UK law is the Data Protection Act 2018, which implements GDPR. Names and dates earn marks.

✗ Giving one-sided 'discuss' answers — a discuss question without both sides AND a conclusion cannot reach the top level, however detailed it is.

✗ Saying open-source software 'has no copyright' — open-source code IS copyrighted; its licence simply grants permission to view, modify and share it under conditions.

✗ Treating environmental impact as disposal only — manufacture (mining, energy, water) and use (electricity, data centres) are equally examinable stages. Structure answers around all three.

✗ Claiming AI 'is biased because programmers are racist' — algorithmic bias usually arises from unrepresentative or historically biased TRAINING DATA, not deliberate intent. Explain the mechanism.

✗ Vague digital divide definitions like 'some people don't have phones' — define it as the gap in access to devices, connectivity AND digital skills, and give a consequence (e.g. exclusion from online-only services).

✗ Forgetting that unauthorised access is illegal even with no damage done — merely viewing data without permission is the first Computer Misuse Act offence.

10. Quick Reference — Key Terms & Revision

★ QUICK REFERENCE — KEY TERMS

E-waste: Discarded electronic devices — contains toxic materials (lead, mercury, cadmium) and recoverable metals

Planned obsolescence: Designing products to have a short useful life so customers replace them sooner

Dematerialisation: Replacing physical products (CDs, paper, tickets) with digital equivalents

Data centre: A facility full of servers powering cloud and internet services — a major consumer of electricity

Privacy: An individual's right to control who collects, sees and uses information about them

Surveillance: Monitoring of people's activity — CCTV, facial recognition, online monitoring

Artificial intelligence (AI): Computers performing tasks that normally require human intelligence

Machine learning (ML): Systems that learn patterns from training data instead of following explicitly programmed rules

Training data: The example data a machine learning system learns from — its quality and representativeness determine the system's fairness

Algorithmic bias: Systematically unfair automated decisions, usually caused by biased or unrepresentative training data

Accountability: The question of who is responsible when an automated system causes harm

Digital divide: The gap between those with access to devices, connectivity and digital skills, and those without

Data Protection Act 2018: UK law (implementing GDPR) governing collection, storage and processing of personal data; gives individuals rights over their data

Computer Misuse Act 1990: UK law criminalising: (1) unauthorised access; (2) unauthorised access with intent to commit further offences; (3) unauthorised modification, including malware

Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988: UK law protecting original works — code, music, images, text — from unauthorised copying or distribution

Intellectual property: Creations of the mind (code, designs, music, brands) owned and controlled by their creator

Software piracy: Copying, distributing or using software without a valid licence

Proprietary software: Closed-source software; users licence the right to use it but cannot view or modify the code (e.g. Windows, Photoshop)

Open-source software: Software whose source code is freely available to view, modify and redistribute under its licence (e.g. Linux, Python, Firefox)
