1. Types of Online Communities [Spec 3.6.1]

📋 SPEC 3.6.1

Understand key features of online communities: social networking, online gaming, online workspaces, VLEs, user-generated reference sites (wikis, websites, forums), user-generated content (video sharing, blogs), social bookmarking.

An online community is any group of people who interact primarily through the internet, sharing common interests, goals or identities. The spec lists seven specific types — know ALL of them.

Social Networking Platforms where users create profiles, connect with others, share content and communicate Features: Friend/follower connections; news feed; messaging; groups; events; photo sharing e.g. Facebook, Instagram, X (Twitter), LinkedIn, Snapchat, TikTok Target: All ages; professionals (LinkedIn); younger users (TikTok/Snapchat)Online Gaming Multiplayer games where players interact, compete and collaborate in real time over the internet Features: Real-time gameplay; voice/text chat; teams; leaderboards; in-game purchases; clans/guilds e.g. Fortnite, Minecraft, FIFA, World of Warcraft, Roblox, Call of Duty Target: Mainly younger people and young adults; genre-dependent audienceOnline Workspaces Platforms enabling teams to collaborate remotely on projects, documents and communication Features: Shared documents; task management; messaging channels; video calls; file sharing e.g. Microsoft Teams, Slack, Google Workspace, Trello, Asana, Basecamp Target: Professionals; remote workers; project teams; businesses of all sizes
Virtual Learning Environments (VLE) Online platforms where educational institutions deliver courses, resources and assessment Features: Course materials; assignments; quizzes; discussion forums; grade tracking; video lectures e.g. Moodle, Blackboard, Canvas, Google Classroom, school intranets Target: Students of all ages; online learners; distance education studentsUser-Generated Reference Sites Websites where users create, edit and maintain informational content collaboratively Features: Open editing; version history; citations; talk pages; community moderation; structured articles e.g. Wikipedia (wiki); Stack Overflow (tech forum); Reddit; Quora; web forums Target: Anyone seeking or sharing knowledge; researchers; developers; studentsUser-Generated Content Sites Platforms where users upload original creative content for others to view, share and comment on Features: Content upload and hosting; view counts; likes/comments; subscriptions; recommendations; monetisation e.g. YouTube (video); Blogger/WordPress (blogs); Tumblr; DeviantArt; SoundCloud Target: Content creators; audiences with specific interests; hobbyists; learners
Social Bookmarking Services where users save, tag, organise and share links to web content they find valuable Features: Bookmark saving; tagging/categorising; public/private sharing; discovering others' bookmarks e.g. Pinterest; Pocket; Diigo; Flipboard; Reddit (link sharing aspect) Target: Researchers; creatives; professionals curating content; avid readers

📌 VLE vs Social Networking — Key Differences

Purpose: VLE is for structured learning delivery. Social networking is for personal connection and entertainment.

Content: VLE: course materials, assignments, grades — structured and curated by teachers. Social media: user-driven, informal, entertainment-focused.

Access: VLE: typically requires login; controlled access (students, teachers, parents). Social media: open to all (usually 13+).

Moderation: VLE: strictly moderated by institution — all content approved. Social media: community-flagged; less controlled.

⚠️ Wikipedia — Useful but Approach with Caution

Good for: Quick overview of a topic; finding links to primary sources; understanding context; checking basic facts.

Not reliable for: Citing as a primary source in academic work; recent or rapidly changing events; niche topics with few editors.

Best practice: Use Wikipedia to FIND sources (check the references at the bottom), then cite THOSE sources rather than Wikipedia itself.

Exam question likely: 'Give ONE advantage and ONE disadvantage of using Wikipedia as an information source.' Advantage: widely covers most topics; quickly updated. Disadvantage: anyone can edit so content may be inaccurate or biased.

6. Acceptable Use & Responsible Behaviour Policies [Spec 3.7.3]

📋 SPEC 3.7.3

Be aware of the purpose of responsible use and acceptable behaviour policies.

Acceptable Use Policies (AUPs) and Responsible Use Policies (RUPs) are formal documents that set out the rules for using a platform, network or device. They protect both the organisation and its users.

Policy ElementWhat It CoversWhy It Exists
Acceptable use definitionWhat the platform/network is and isn't designed for — permitted and prohibited activitiesEstablishes clear expectations; reduces misuse; provides legal basis for enforcement

💡 Why AUPs Matter — School Example

School network AUP covers: Permitted use of school devices; banned websites; password security; no sharing of personal information; no bullying via school systems.

Consequence of breach: Warning → suspension of network access → permanent ban → referral to parents → possible exclusion for serious breaches.

Exam question: 'Why do schools have acceptable use policies for their computer networks?' Answer: To protect users from harmful or inappropriate content (1); to set clear expectations for appropriate use (1); to provide a legal basis for taking action against misuse (1); to protect the school from legal liability (1).

7. Online Safety Issues [Spec 3.7.4]

📋 SPEC 3.7.4

Understand online safety: cyberbullying, anonymity of others (misrepresentation), disclosure of personal information/location.

The internet presents specific risks, especially for younger users. You must know the four key safety issues — what they are, how they happen, their impact and how to protect against them.

Cyberbullying Repeated use of digital technology to bully, intimidate or humiliate a specific person How: Social media harassment; nasty comments; excluding from online groups; sharing humiliating images; creating fake profiles Impact: Severe psychological harm; anxiety; depression; self-harm; school absence; social isolation Protect: Report and block; screenshot evidence; tell a trusted adult; platform reporting tools; school anti-bullying policyAnonymity & Misrepresentation People online are not always who they claim to be — they can hide their true identity How: Fake profiles with false photos; pretending to be younger or older; impersonating celebrities or authority figures Impact: Trust betrayed; potential grooming; fraud; false information spread; manipulation Protect: Never share personal details with online strangers; video call to verify identity; tell an adult about suspicious contactsDisclosure of Personal Information Sharing private details online that could be used by malicious actors How: Sharing address, school, daily routine, phone number; location tagging in photos Impact: Risk of physical danger; stalking; identity theft; grooming by predators Protect: Privacy settings on all accounts; never share address or location; think before posting; check geotagging settings
Location Disclosure Revealing current or regular locations through posts, check-ins, geotagged photos How: Instagram location tags; Snapchat maps; check-ins on Facebook; EXIF data in photos Impact: Real-world safety risk; burglars can see when home is empty; predators can locate victims Protect: Disable geotagging in camera settings; never post 'I'm on holiday for 2 weeks'; turn off location sharing in apps

🛡️ Responding to Cyberbullying — Action Plan

1. Don't respond: Reacting gives the bully what they want — attention. Ignore and avoid escalation.

2. Screenshot evidence: Document every incident with screenshots and timestamps before blocking or deleting.

3. Block and report: Use the platform's block and report features — this removes the bully's ability to contact you.

4. Tell someone: Tell a trusted adult (parent, teacher, carer) — you should never deal with cyberbullying alone.

5. Contact the platform: Most platforms take bullying seriously and will remove accounts and content that violate their AUP.

6. Police if serious: Threats, sharing of intimate images without consent and sustained harassment can be criminal offences.

⚡ EXAM TIP

Cyberbullying question structure: 'Describe ONE way a young person could protect themselves from cyberbullying.' Name the action → explain how it helps → link to the specific risk. e.g. 'The young person should set their social media account to private (1). This restricts who can see their posts and contact them, so the bully cannot continue to access their profile or send messages (1).'

📌 Selecting the Right Source for the Task

Scientific research: Use peer-reviewed journals (Nature, PubMed, IEEE Xplore) — not blogs or news summaries.

Health information: Use NHS.uk, WHO.int or reputable medical organisations — not forums or personal anecdotes.

Current events: Use BBC, Reuters, AP News — verify across multiple outlets; avoid relying on a single source.

Legal matters: Use gov.uk for UK law; official legislation websites; avoid lay interpretation on forums.

General research starting point: Wikipedia for overview and references only — then follow the citations to primary sources.

Exam rule: Always select sources that match the PURPOSE. A question about 'should you use social media for research?' requires you to explain WHY it is often unreliable.

💡 How Search Engines Work — Brief Overview

Crawling: Automated bots ('crawlers' or 'spiders') visit billions of web pages and follow links to discover new pages.

Indexing: Pages are analysed — content, keywords, links and structure — and stored in a massive searchable index.

Ranking: When you search, an algorithm (over 200 factors for Google) ranks results by estimated relevance and credibility.

PageRank: Pages with more links from other reputable pages rank higher — links act as 'votes of credibility'.

SEO: Search Engine Optimisation — techniques website owners use to make their pages rank higher (can distort results).

Filter bubble: Search engines personalise results based on your history — you may not see different viewpoints, creating a 'bubble'.

10. Evaluating Information [Spec 3.9.4]

📋 SPEC 3.9.4

Evaluate fitness for purpose in terms of: accuracy, age, relevance, reliability, bias.

Finding information is easy — evaluating whether it is trustworthy and appropriate for your purpose is the critical skill. Use the five-criteria CRAAB framework below.

ACCURACY Is the information factually correct?Check: Cross-reference with multiple independent sources; look for supporting data/evidence; check for factual errors Warning signs: Statistics without source; claims contradicted by other reputable sources
AGE (Currency) How recent is the information?Check: Check publication date; look for 'last updated' note; consider whether the topic changes rapidly Warning signs: Undated content; scientific/tech info older than 5 years; no update history
RELEVANCE Does this information actually address the question?Check: Does it match your research question? Is the scope appropriate? Is it pitched at the right level? Warning signs: Tangentially related; aimed at a different audience; covers a different aspect of the topic
RELIABILITY Can the source be trusted?Check: Who wrote it? What are their credentials? Does the site have editorial standards? Are sources cited? Warning signs: Anonymous author; no citations; no editorial oversight; self-published with no credentials
BIAS Is the information objective or does it push a particular viewpoint?Check: Who funds/owns the site? Is both sides of an argument presented? Is emotive language used? Warning signs: Content from organisations with a stake in the outcome; one-sided presentation; political spin
🔍 CRAAB Framework — Checklist for Evaluating Any Website

C — Currency: When was this written? Is there a date? For science and technology, information older than 3–5 years may be outdated.

R — Relevance: Does this actually answer my question? Is the audience the same as mine? Is the scope right?

A — Authority: Who wrote this? What are their qualifications? Is there editorial oversight? Is the site's identity clear?

A — Accuracy: Is the information supported by evidence? Are claims referenced? Does it match other reliable sources?

B — Bias: Who funds this site? Is there a commercial, political or ideological motive? Is the language objective or emotive?

Before trusting any source: Ask ALL FIVE questions. A page may score well on accuracy but badly on bias (e.g. a well-researched but commercially funded study).

11. Copyright in the Digital World [Spec 3.9.5]

📋 SPEC 3.9.5

Understand copyright issues: permission to use, acknowledgement of source.

Copyright protects creators' original works automatically from the moment of creation. Online content — text, images, videos, music, code — is almost always protected by copyright, even when freely accessible.

📋 Copyright Fundamentals for Digital Content

Automatic protection: No registration needed. Copyright applies the moment a creative work is created — the copyright symbol (©) is optional.

Duration: In the UK/EU: 70 years after the author's death. For films and music, calculated differently.

'Free online' ≠ 'free to use': Images, articles and videos are still copyrighted even when not behind a paywall.

Licence types: All Rights Reserved (must ask permission) │ Creative Commons (specific permissions granted) │ Public Domain (free to use)

Fair dealing (UK) / Fair use (USA): Limited exceptions allow use of copyrighted material for criticism, commentary, parody, education — but not wholesale copying.

Attribution: Even when permitted to use, always acknowledge the source: title, creator, date, URL/publication.

12. Plagiarism [Spec 3.9.6]

📋 SPEC 3.9.6

Understand plagiarism: copy and paste, rewriting, paraphrasing.

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's work or ideas as your own without proper credit. The spec explicitly mentions three forms — copy and paste, rewriting, and paraphrasing — all of which constitute plagiarism when the source is not acknowledged.

FeaturePlagiarismCopyright Infringement
What it isPresenting someone else's work or ideas as your own — without giving creditUsing someone else's protected work without their permission
Legal issue?Not always illegal — but always academically dishonest and unethicalYES — always illegal; violates intellectual property law
ExamplesCopying a paragraph from Wikipedia into coursework without citation; paraphrasing without creditDownloading a film without buying it; using a copyrighted image without a licence
Paraphrasing counts?YES — rewriting someone else's ideas without crediting the source is still plagiarismParaphrasing text does not infringe copyright, but presenting it as own work is plagiarism
ConsequencesAcademic failure; expulsion; damaged reputation; professional misconductLawsuit; fines; criminal prosecution; takedown notices; damages payment
PreventionAlways cite sources; use your own words and add your own analysis; use plagiarism checkersGet proper licences; use Creative Commons material; check usage rights before use

12.1 Examples: What Counts as Plagiarism?

ApproachExampleIs This Acceptable?
Direct Copy (no credit)Typed verbatim: "Climate change is the long-term shift in global temperatures and weather patterns."✗ PLAGIARISM — original words used without quotation marks or attribution
Direct Copy (with quote and credit)"Climate change is the long-term shift in global temperatures and weather patterns." (NASA, 2023)✓ ACCEPTABLE — original words clearly quoted AND source credited
Paraphrase (no credit)Rewritten as: 'Global warming involves changes in weather and temperature over a long period.'✗ PLAGIARISM — different words but same ideas without crediting the source
Paraphrase (with credit)As NASA (2023) explains, climate change refers to long-term shifts in global weather and temperature patterns.✓ ACCEPTABLE — ideas paraphrased AND source credited
Copy and paste from internetDownloaded article text pasted directly into assignment without any change or acknowledgement✗ SERIOUS PLAGIARISM — most detectable form; caught by software like Turnitin instantly
Synthesis and citationMultiple sources compared and combined, with each cited: 'According to NASA (2023) and the IPCC (2022), climate change...'✓ BEST PRACTICE — uses multiple sources; adds own analysis; cites all
📋 How to Avoid Plagiarism — Best Practice

1. QUOTE + CITE: If using exact words, put them in quotation marks and add a citation: 'The internet has transformed communication' (Berners-Lee, 2020).

2. PARAPHRASE + CITE: If rewording an idea, you still must cite the source even though the words are different.

3. SYNTHESISE: The best academic practice — use MULTIPLE sources, compare them, and add YOUR OWN analysis.

4. USE A REFERENCE LIST: At the end of any work, list all sources used — even if not directly quoted.

5. TURNITIN CHECK: Many institutions use plagiarism detection software. Even paraphrased content can be detected.

Key rule: If an idea came from somewhere other than your own mind, you must acknowledge where it came from — always.

Q: A student is researching information about climate change for a school project and finds two websites: (A) a NASA government website updated in 2024 and (B) a blog post from 2019 with no author name or references. Explain which source is more suitable and give THREE reasons. [6 marks]

Q: Explain what is meant by plagiarism. Describe TWO forms of plagiarism that are possible when using the internet for research. [4 marks]

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's ideas, words or work as your own without acknowledging the original source (1). First form: copy and paste plagiarism — copying text directly from a website and pasting it into an essay without quotation marks or a citation (1), making it appear as though the student wrote those words themselves when they did not (1). Second form: paraphrasing without citation — rewriting a source in different words but presenting it without crediting the original author (1). Even though the words are different, the ideas are still the source author's and must be acknowledged (1).

Why this scores full marks: ✓ Definition clear: someone else's work presented as own, without acknowledgement ✓ First form: specific action (copy-paste) + why it is plagiarism (appears as own work) ✓ Second form: paraphrasing — importantly explains that different words still require citation ✓ Five distinct marking points in total — definition (1) + 2 marks per form (2 × 2 = 4) = 5 points for 4 marks; any 4 would score

14. Common Mistakes

✗ COMMON MISTAKES

✗ Naming only 'social media' as a type of online community — the spec lists seven distinct types. Always use the correct specific term: 'social networking site', 'online workspace', 'virtual learning environment', etc.

✗ Saying 'paraphrasing is not plagiarism' — WRONG. Paraphrasing someone else's ideas without crediting the source IS plagiarism, even when the words are completely different. The source of the idea must always be acknowledged.

✗ Thinking 'free online' means 'free to use' — all online content is copyright-protected unless explicitly stated otherwise (Creative Commons or public domain). You cannot use images from Google search without checking the licence.

✗ Describing Wikipedia as 'reliable' without qualification — Wikipedia is useful as a starting point but should never be cited as a primary source. Open editing means any entry may contain errors or bias.

✗ Confusing cyberbullying with general online rudeness — cyberbullying is REPEATED, TARGETED harassment. A single offensive comment is not cyberbullying. The repetitive nature and specific targeting are key characteristics.

✗ Saying anonymity is always a bad thing — anonymity also enables whistleblowing, reporting abuse safely, and political dissent in repressive countries. Questions may ask for advantages AND disadvantages.

✗ Giving only one evaluation criterion when asked about assessing a website — the spec lists five: accuracy, age (currency), relevance, reliability and bias. Learn all five with the CRAAB mnemonic.

✗ Forgetting to mention acknowledgement of source in copyright questions — two components are always needed: (1) permission to use AND (2) acknowledgement of source. Citing a source does NOT give you permission to use it.

✗ Describing ALL online communities as having the same purpose — each type has a specific primary function. Online gaming ≠ social networking ≠ VLE ≠ wiki. Know the distinction clearly.

✗ Not linking cyberbullying answers to specific platforms — always give an example platform where the specific form of bullying occurs (e.g. 'impersonation via a fake Instagram profile' not just 'online').

16. Quick Reference — Key Terms & Revision

★ KEY TERMS — QUICK REFERENCE

Online Community: A group of people who interact with each other primarily through the internet, sharing common interests, goals or identities

Social Networking: Online platforms where users create profiles, connect with others and share content (e.g. Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn)

Virtual Learning Environment (VLE): An online platform used by educational institutions to deliver courses, resources, assignments and communication (e.g. Moodle, Google Classroom)

Wiki: A website that allows anyone to create, edit and update content collaboratively — Wikipedia is the largest example

Social Bookmarking: A service where users save and share links to web content, organised by tags (e.g. Pinterest, Pocket)

User-Generated Content: Content created and uploaded by users rather than professional publishers (e.g. YouTube videos, blog posts)

Acceptable Use Policy (AUP): A set of rules governing how a platform or network may be used — defines permitted and prohibited behaviour

Cyberbullying: Repeated use of digital technology to bully, threaten, humiliate or exclude a specific person

Anonymity: The state of being unknown — online anonymity allows people to interact without revealing their real identity

Misrepresentation: Presenting false information about oneself online — e.g. using a false name, age or photo

Doxing: Publishing someone's private personal information (address, phone, workplace) online without their consent

Plagiarism: Presenting someone else's work, ideas or words as your own without appropriate acknowledgement

Copyright Infringement: Using someone else's protected creative work without permission or a valid licence

Citation: Acknowledging the source of information, ideas or quotes used in your own work

Paraphrasing: Rewording someone else's ideas in your own words — still requires citation to avoid plagiarism

Accuracy: Whether information is factually correct — must be verified against reliable, independent sources

Currency (Age): How recent information is — important for fast-changing topics like technology, science and current events

Reliability: Whether a source can be trusted — depends on author credentials, editorial oversight and transparency

Bias: A tendency to present information in a way that favours a particular point of view or outcome

CRAAP Test: A framework for evaluating information: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose

Responsible Use Policy: Rules for appropriate and safe use of technology, typically in schools or workplaces

Creative Commons: A licensing framework that allows creators to grant specific usage rights to the public (e.g. CC-BY allows free use with attribution)

⚡ 7 Online Community Types — Quick Revision

Social Networking: Connect with people; share updates; messaging — Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, TikTok

Online Gaming: Multiplayer real-time games with chat; teams; competition — Fortnite, Minecraft, Roblox

Online Workspaces: Remote professional collaboration; documents; tasks; video calls — Teams, Slack, Google Workspace

VLE: Educational platform for courses; assignments; grades; forums — Moodle, Google Classroom

User-Generated Reference: Collaboratively written knowledge base; open editing — Wikipedia, Stack Overflow, forums

User-Generated Content: Upload and share creative content; comments; subscriptions — YouTube, Blogger, SoundCloud

Social Bookmarking: Save, tag and share links to web content; curate collections — Pinterest, Pocket, Reddit

⚡ CRAAB — Information Evaluation Quick Reference

C — Currency: How recent? Check publication/update date. Important for science, tech, current events.

R — Relevance: Does it address your actual question at the right level and scope?

A — Authority: Who wrote it? What are their credentials? Is there editorial oversight?

A — Accuracy: Is it factually correct? Supported by evidence? Cross-verified with other sources?

B — Bias: Who funds/owns the site? Is it presenting only one side? Is language objective or emotive?

Plagiarism: Copy-paste (no citation) │ Paraphrase (no citation) │ Rewrite (no citation) = ALL plagiarism

Not plagiarism: Direct quote + citation │ Paraphrase + citation │ Own synthesis + all sources cited
