## Narration (full text)

Long ago, in a garden called Lumbini, a prince was born, named Siddhartha. A wise man foretold, he would become a great king, or a great teacher. His father wanted a king, so he hid every sorrow behind the palace walls. Siddhartha grew up among music, gardens, and endless spring. But one day, he rode out beyond the gates. He saw an old man, bent with age. A sick man, shaking with fever. And a silent procession, carrying someone who would never wake. Then he saw a wandering monk, who owned nothing, and yet was at peace. That night, he looked once at his sleeping wife and son, and quietly left the palace. He cut off his long hair, and traded his silks for a simple robe. For six years he searched, fasting until he was thin as a reed. But starving the body did not free the mind. So he accepted a bowl of rice milk from a village girl, and chose the middle path. He sat beneath a great fig tree, and vowed not to rise until he understood. Mara, lord of illusion, sent storms, and armies, and fear. He simply touched the earth. And as the morning star rose, he awoke. He was the Buddha, the awakened one. For forty five years he walked from village to village, teaching kindness, and a calm mind. In many traditions, he is honoured as the ninth avatar of Vishnu, a reminder that peace is not found in palaces, but within.

## Notes on the telling

- The life story follows the traditional account (Buddhacarita, Nidanakatha): birth at Lumbini, the seer's prophecy, the sheltered palace, the four sights, the night departure, six years of austerity, Sujata's bowl of rice milk, the Bodhi tree, Mara, the earth-touching gesture, the awakening at the rising of the morning star, and forty-five years of teaching.
- The Buddha is counted as the ninth avatar of Vishnu in many Hindu traditions (for example the Bhagavata Purana and Jayadeva's Dashavatara hymn); the narration says "in many traditions" because some lists name Balarama instead, and Buddhists do not regard the Buddha as an avatar.
- Death is shown only as a distant, silhouetted procession.
